          Interactive Crossovers:

          Jeremy Wilkey defines the second type of crossover as an interactive crossover. Interactive crossovers use a shim diameter that is larger than the stack clamp. When the crossover is larger than the clamp some of the force on the face shims is transferred directly through the crossover shim into the high speed stack. The transferred force causes the high speed stack to start deflecting before the crossover gap closes. Mog's stack dyno tested by MXScandinavia on a four port Ohlins valve is an example of an interactive crossover.

          To close the crossover gap the face shims have to “chase down” the moving/deflecting high speed stack on an interactive crossover. That delays closure of the crossover gap and spreads out the crossover closure “event” over a range of shaft velocities. Mog's stack shows those effects. There is no kink in the stack deflection curve when the crossover gap closes and no kink in the damping force curve measured on the dyno for that stack.

          Interactive crossovers have no distinct “event” when the crossover gap closes. Instead, the process is spread out over a range of shaft velocities. That introduces the idea of “mid-speed” damping where the damping force is somewhere in between the low speed force, defined by the face shims, and high speed force defined by the stiffness of the entire stack.

           

          Why do we see so few of these (non-interactive crossovers) types of stacks ?they seem better focused at the individual stages of ls and hs , I've only seem one oem with this a 04 rm

           

          Why so few non-interactive crossovers?

          Non-interactive crossovers have a well defined low speed and high speed stack separated by the crossover gap. That makes a lot of tuning sense, but creates a sharp transition when the crossover closes. Jeremy Wilkey points out that creates a shock that works “well” on one series of bumps but on the next series of slightly larger bumps the crossover closes and the shock suddenly gets too stiff.  

           

          The fix is an interactive crossover to spread out the transition and produce a more consistent feel across the shaft velocity range. That's the theory – in practice tuning gets tricky.

          Cool to see theory work out in practice once in awhile...

           

          Above, Jeremy Wilkey defined two basic types of crossovers: Interactive and non-interactive. Beyond those basic configurations the dyno data on TT demonstrates use of two additional types of crossovers: Trapped crossovers and “fo” crossovers.

           

          Trapped Crossovers:

          Instead of a single shim, trapped crossovers use a pair of shims with different diameters to form the crossover gap. As the crossover closes the larger shim gets “trapped” in the crossover gap. That causes the face shims to start out with a small clamp diameter and shift to a larger clamp when the second shim gets trapped in the closing crossover gap. The change in clamp diameter causes the low speed damping start out soft and transition to a stiffer rate as the crossover closes.

          MXScandinavia dyno testing of the Jake Normal stack demonstrates use of a trapped crossover.

          "fo" Crossovers:

          The fourth type of crossover is a “fo” crossover. “fo” crossovers, like “fo” fur, are fake and create a crossover gap that never closes. “fo” crossovers are generated by crossover shim diameters that are too large or high speed stacks that are too soft. When the crossover diameter is too large too much force is transferred through the crossover into the high speed stack. That forces the high speed stack to deflect at the same rate as the face shims so the “fo” gap never closes. “fo” crossovers do nothing other than cause confusion. MXScandinavia shows some examples.

          “fo” gaps are sensitive to changes in the shim stack configuration. For example on the above stack: If you reduce the crossover shim diameter a tad, less force is transferred through the crossover into the high speed stack. That reduces the high speed stack deflection and converts what was a “fo” crossover into a regular interactive crossover.

          Crossovers that flip into and out of a “fo” configuration drive dyno tuners crazy. With only minor changes to the stack taper or clamp a crossover can flop over into a “fo” configuration causing the whole damping force curve to flop over into a different shape – often by a bunch.

          Those sudden shifts in the stack behavior lead to all kinds of wacky dyno based conclusions like non-linear shim forces or complex three dimensional fluid dynamics dominating the shocks performance. Worse, it prevents you from getting the damping profile you wanted, not because the shim stack was bad, but because things were going on in the stack nobody understood when dyno testing the shock. “fo” crossovers flipping into and out of an active configuration can wasting weeks of dyno test time trying to figure out what is going on in the stack.

          Non-Interactive Crossover Configurations

          The dyno data on TT shows some of examples of non-interactive crossovers. We'll take a look at a few here and more latter comparing the effects of different crossover styles.  

           

          crf450r Dogger rebound

          John Curea, using the Mx Tech dyno, ran a couple of dyno tests helping the Dogger setup his crf450r rebound stack on the Showa 50 oval port valve used in that shock. The Dogger's rebound stack had a conventional non-interactive crossover with a shim diameter smaller than the stack clamp. The configuration allows the face shims to deflect independently from the high speed stack. The stack deflection curve shows the behavior you would expect for a non-interactive crossover: Soft at low speed until the crossover closes and a sudden transition to a stiffer rate after that point.  

          Normally, the stack face shims hold the valve port closed at low speed. When the valve port is closed all of the flow is forced through the clicker bleed circuits driving low speed damping forces up. But, the Dogger's stack only had four face shims. The soft face shims allowed the valve port to blow open at low speed so the flow bypassed the clicker bleed circuits. The end result is the stack produced little low speed damping making a damping force curve that was nearly linear all the way down to a shaft speed of zero.

          So is this desirable ?a linear force curve ?

           

          No, a linear damping curve is not desirable. Agree with tmsuspension.

           

          Here's the theory:

          250 lb bike, 180 lb rider, 40/60 weight split. Take off 50 lbs for the front wheel and another 50 for the rear and the rear chassis weight is around 200 lbs. At 200 lbs the chassis needs some stiff damping for motion control, but at the same time the 50 lb wheels need to float over bumps.

           

          The shock's performance has to simultaneously satisfy two conflicting requirements: Stiff damping to control the chassis and soft damping to float the wheel over bumps. The shock can't do both, it is one or the other.

           

          The usual compromise is tune the shock to produce stiff damping at low speed for chassis control, and soft damping at high speed to control the wheels. That creates a damping profile with a “knee” in the curve marking the point where the shock transitions from low speed chassis damping to high speed wheel damping. Wipe out the knee and the chassis becomes a wallowing mess. Wrap your head around that and you will go far in suspension tuning.

           

          That's the basic theory. But on the other hand, the wheel has to punch through all of that low speed damping every time you hit a bump. Too much low speed and the ride gets harsh – not enough and the chassis wallows. Suspension tuning is all about controlling that transition and positioning the “knee” to generate enough low speed damping to control the chassis and transitioning out of that range fast enough so the ride doesn't get harsh.

           

          On the rebound side harsh means packing. Most rebound stacks use a crossover to back off on the low speed a bit and prevent packing. Go to stiffer springs and the low speed damping can be increased a bit without packing up the rebound stroke -or stated the other way – stiffer springs require stiffer rebound to keep control of the chassis and the wheels as well.

          John Curea Stack Mod For Stiffer Spring Rate

          Based on the shim stack configuration and dyno test Curea suspected the rebound was too soft and recommended two mods: Increase low speed damping to handle the Dogger's 270 lb weight; and increase high speed damping to handle the Dogger's stiffer spring rates.

           

          To get more low speed damping Curea added five more face shims. The stiffer face shims kept the valve ports closed longer forcing the flow to be “on the clickers” at low speed to increase low speed damping. At high speed Curea wanted 10% more rebound damping to handle the stiffer springs. To get a 10% high speed increase Curea had to actually soften the high speed stack to compensate for the stiffer face shims. Curea's dyno test shows the combination of softer high speed and stiffer low speed results in an overall high speed damping force increase of 10%.

          The stiffer stack face shims John Curea used moved the crossover closure point out to 5 in/sec compared to 3 in/sec for the Dogger's original setup. That increased the low speed damping by about 40% at 5 in/sec. At high speed the softer clamp used in Curea's stack softened the high speed damping so the overall increase at high speed was only about 10%. 

           

          Earlier, MXScandinavia tested interactive crossover stacks and showed increasing the low speed stack stiffness increased the damping force everywhere, both at low speed and at high speed. Likewise, increasing the high speed stack stiffness increased damping force everywhere across the speed range. In this test Curea shows that if you stiffen the low speed stack AND soften the high speed stack you can actually change the low speed damping force curve relative to the high speed. But you've got to simultaneously change both parts of the stack to make that work. That gets confusing if you've got a “one thing at a time” approach to shim stack tuning.

           

          The other thing John Curea shows in this test is the behavior of the crossover gap changed when the stack got re-tuned. The shaft velocity where the crossover gap closed got shifted and the abruptness or “kink” in the stack deflection curve got smoothed out. Same crossover, same crossover gap, but the two stacks are going to “feel” different on a test ride with the crossover gap transition smoothed out.

          John Curea yz250

          Curea posted a second non-interactive crossover example for a stock yz250 shock. In this case the crossover shim diameter is slightly larger than the stack clamp. That makes the configuration an interactive crossover by Jeremy Wilkey's definition. But in this case, the low speed stack is soft and the high speed stack is stiff. So the forces transferred through the crossover shim are not enough to start deflection the high speed stack before the crossover closes. You can see that in the stack deflection plot. Since the high speed stack does not deflect until the crossover closes the configuration basically behaves like a non-interactive crossover.  

          You'd like to have hard and fast rules for shim stack tuning like the definitions of interactive and non-interactive crossover gaps. But, shim stack behavior often ends up being an “it depends” kind of deal. For the above stack, the stiffness of the low speed stack relative to the high speed stack turned out to be more important then the small diameter difference between the crossover and stack clamp. So the stack ends up behaving like a regular non-interactive crossover instead of an interactive crossover. The dyno test above is one of those examples where one parameter ends up dominating over another and the stack behavior ends up being different than the hard and fast rules you might of had in mind when assembling the stack.

          Valving Logic rmz250 Shock Compression:

          Valving Logic has also posted examples of non-interactive crossovers on TT. This rmz250 shock compression stack was run on a kyb 46mm valve. The stack has a crossover diameter slightly larger than the stack clamp, so by the numbers the stack was an interactive crossover configuration. But the stack deflection plot does not show any deflection of the high speed stack at the point where the crossover closes. So the stack actually behaves like a non-interactive crossover.

           

          Either way, the change in stack stiffness at the point where the crossover closes was small and did not produce any real change in damping force measured for the shock. In that respect the test data shows it did not really matter whether the crossover was an interactive or non-interactive configuration.

          The idea of the crossover shim diameter defining the difference between active and non-interactive crossover gaps is straight forward. Implementing that in practice gets tricky because the crossover gap behavior depends on the crossover shim diameter and the stiffness of the low speed stack relative to the high speed stack. The last three dyno tests show examples where an interactive crossover gap configuration ended up testing out as non-interactive gap because the low speed stack was much softer than the high speed stack. So you have to consider both the crossover gap configuration and stack stiffness to figure out how a crossover gap is going to behave.

           

          Stack Float

          One more non-interactive crossover gap configuration from Kawamaha. This stack is for a yz250 fork compression valve. The stack uses a very thick crossover shim with a diameter that is larger than the stack clamp. That makes the stack a potential interactive crossover configuration. But, the crossover shim is very thick and stiff so it does not bend at all. That makes the configuration behave like a regular non-interactive crossover.

           

          Looking at the shim stack configuration plot it is obvious the crossover shim in that stack is not going to bend. That is not so obvious if you are just looking at a listing of shims used in the stack.

          The above shim stack also has float. Because of the float and the crossover gap there are two events in the dyno curve. One when the stack float opens against the check spring and a second when the crossover gap closes. Those events put two inflection points in the dyno curve.
