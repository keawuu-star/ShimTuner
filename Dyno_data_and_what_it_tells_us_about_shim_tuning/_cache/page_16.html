          Crossover Closure Events:

          When a crossover closes why do stack stiffness curves show a sharp transition while damping force curves do not?

           

          FEA stack edge lift curves measure shim stack lift at valve port center line. At that location the face shims freely deflect across the crossover gap until contacting the high speed stack. After contact, further deflection requires a bunch more force to deflect both the low speed and high speed stack. FEA curves show a sharp transition at that point because there is in fact a sharp transition when the high speed stack starts deflecting. Knowing where that point occurs is useful for tuning.

           

          Damping force, on the other hand, does not care very much about stack edge lift. Damping force depends on fluid flow resistance and that depends on flow area. At the point where the crossover closes the flow area curve behaves a little differently than stack edge lift.  

          When the crossover closes the face shims contact the high speed stack, further increases in force cause the low speed stack to mash out against the high speed stack. Mashing the face shims against the high speed stack opens the left and right hand sides of the valve port. That increase the flow area while the center line lift of the shim stack hardly changes at all. The flow area increase from opening the sides of the valve port makes the flow area curve look a whole lot different than stack edge lift.

          FEA calculations compute that process of mashing the low speed stack against the high speed stack, but to see that effect you have to look at flow area – not stack lift. Flow area is a PIA to compute because the shim stack is bent in both the radial and left-right tangential directions. Computing flow area requires integrating all the way around the valve port perimeter measuring stack lift at each location around the valve seat perimeter to sum up the total area. Many FEA analyzes skip that step and just report edge lift because that is easy to measure.

           

          Flow area curves show there is no sharp transition when the crossover closes. The shim stack smoothly opens the left and right hand sides of the valve port as the face shims mash out against the high speed stack. The smooth transition in flow area smooths damping force so there is no distinct “event” when the crossover closes. The shim stack is clearly stiffer after the crossover closes but that transition is spread out over a range of shaft velocities. That process is described by FEA calculations but you have to look at flow area – not edge lift.

           

          The transition when the crossover closes is further smoothed by flow through the bleed circuits. The fluid flow through the shock's internal flow circuits is not stupid. When the crossover closes the fluid does not sit there and hammer away against the stiffer high speed stack. Instead the fluid takes the path of least resistance rerouting itself through the bleed circuits to slowly build pressure and deflect the stiffer high speed stack. Shifting flow through the bleed circuits further rounds off the crossover closure event smoothing the crossover closure transition.

           

          Tuners occasionally rag on FEA calculations as useless info because the sharp shim stack transitions computed by FEA don't show up in “real world” damping force data. They are simply misinformed or too lazy to figure out the difference between edge lift and flow area. FEA stack deflection calculations show a smoothed transition as the low speed stack mashes out against the high speed stack – that results in a smoothed damping force curve. Edge lift on-the-other-hand shows a sharp transition because there is a sharp stiffness change when the crossover closes. That sharp stiffness change is useful to identify where the crossover closes, but it is also important to understand the flow area curves look different.

          Stack Deflection Measurements

          We started down this bunny trail looking at crossover data as a way to measure shim stack lift. Turns out the process of mashing the low speed stack against the high speed stack smooths the crossover transition to the point where there is no distinct event. The point where the crossover closes is too subtle to pick out of dyno data in a convincing way.

           

          Now that we know more about how a crossover closes, it's useful to go back and look at some of the other data measuring shim stack lift.

           

          Finger Press: A finger press mechanically deflects the stack by finger posts stuck directly through the valve port. That gives a direct measure of shim stack lift, but as we now know, stack edge lift is not directly related to damping force. To understand damping force you need flow area and flow area depends on how the low speed stack mashes out against the high speed stack to open the left and right hand sides of the valve port. Narrow ports open quickly, wide valve ports spread the process out as it takes longer to fully mash out the low speed stack. Finger press measurements of stack center line lift aren't going to give you that data.  

           

          Backer Limit Tests: Put a stiff backer behind the stack and you can use the dyno to see where the shim stack hits the backer. That deflects the shim stack in a natural way using the fluid force of the shock. At the point where the stack hits the backer, the stack begins mashing out against the backer slowly open the left and right hand sides of the valve port. That softens the closure event. The point where the stack hit the backer is where the stiffness increase started – not fished; and that process looks different on a narrow valve port or a wide valve port.  

           

          Crossover Testing: Dyno data clearly shows the shim stack is stiffer after the crossover closes. But the transition is spread out over a range of shaft velocities with no clear indication of where the crossover closure started or finished. The process is too subtle to get any direct, convincing measurement of shim stack lift out of dyno crossover testing.

           

          FEA Calculations: FEA calculations load shim stacks in different ways: edge loaded; point loaded; and fluid loaded. You need fluid loaded calculations to describe now the low speed stack mashes out against the high speed stack. Port center line lift measurements are useful to spot where a crossover closes, but to understand damping force you have to go through the additional step of extracting flow area from the calculations. Center line lift measurements are not enough to understand how the shim stack is going to perform.

          Shaft diameter effect on shock performance

          • russ17: "If you switch from a 16mm shaft to an 18mm shaft does the damping force go up or down?"

          Here's the theory:

          • On the compression stroke a larger shaft diameter pushes more flow through the compression adjuster (base valve) into the shock oil reservoir. That drives damping force up.
          • But, the larger shaft also takes up more volume in the rebound chamber so there is less oil flow through the mid-valve. That drives damping force down.

          Does a larger shaft diameter drive damping force up or down? Skoo asked that same question for a snowmobile shock.

           

           

          John Curea took up the challenge and dyno tested a '06 yz250 46mm kyb shock with a 16 mm and 18 mm shaft to answer russ17's question.

          • Same shock, same valve, same compression adjuster

          • Same shim stack

          • Only change was 16 mm to 18 mm shaft

          • Does a larger shaft diameter drive damping force up or down?

          The 18mm shaft pushes more fluid into the oil reservoir compressing the bladder more which increases the gas spring force measured on the dyno. To compare the 16mm shaft data to the 18mm data we need to correct the dyno measurements for the difference in gas spring force for the two shafts.

           

          All of the dyno data to this point has been PVP (peak velocity pickoff) data. PVP testing only records data at the peak shaft velocity. To get a damping force curve you have to run a series of dyno tests at progressively higher crank rpms to generate progressively higher shaft speeds only recording data at the mid-stroke position where shaft speeds are the highest. The feature of PVP testing is all of the measurements are obtained at the mid-stroke position so the gas reservoir pressure is theoretically always the same – ignoring oil temperature changes over the test which of coarse change the gas pressure.

           

          John Curea ran a CVP dyno test. CVP data (Continuous Velocity Plot) sets the dyno to run at the max shaft speed and records data over a single shock stroke. The test starts at BDC with zero shaft velocity and finishes at TDC with zero shaft velocity. Over the stroke oil is pushed into the reservoir so the final reservoir pressure at TDC is higher than the initial pressure at BDC. That creates a situation where the data recorded through the first half of the stroke does not line up with data through the second half of the stroke because of the increase in reservoir gas pressure. The change in gas force over the stroke creates a hysteresis loop in CVP dyno data.

           

          Ordinarily dyno tuners ignore the gas force change between BDC and TDC and just subtract the mid-stroke gas force as an “average” value over the stroke. That is close enough for most applications but we are trying to make some detailed comparisons here so lets make the correction one time and see how it works out. To make the correction we need to track how much oil is transferred into the reservoir as a function of stroke position, compute the gas force change, and subtract the gas force increase at each stroke position. That has been worked out in the figure below showing the original and gas force corrected data.  

          Correcting the dyno data for changes in gas force over the stroke pretty much eliminates the hysteresis loop measured on the rebound stroke. Good to know.

           

          On the rebound stroke, the difference between those curves is caused by the fluid flow rate difference between a 16mm and 18mm shaft. How chose is the data to the expected effect?

          Theoretical effect on rebound damping

          Dyno testing shows the 18mm shaft produced less rebound damping. That was expected since the larger 18mm shaft has less oil flow through the valve. How close are the dyno results to the expected effect?

           

          The oil volume swept through the valve is straight forward to calculate.

                

          An 18 mm shaft sweeps an oil volume that is 3.8% less than a 16 mm shaft. That creates two effects reducing rebound damping force:  

          • At any shaft velocity, the 18 mm shaft has 3.8% less oil flow through the valve. If you compared the 18mm data to the 16mm data at a shaft velocity that is 3.8% lower the oil flow through the 16mm valve would be identical

          • The pressure drop across the valve is the damping force measured on the dyno. That force is created by the pressure difference between the top and bottom surface of the valve. Since the 18 mm shaft has 3.8% less exposed valve area the shock is going to generate 3.8% less damping force when the pressure drop across the valve is the same

          Both of those are simple geometric effects created by the shaft diameter change. If you corrected for the oil flow differences and the valve face area change you should be able to overlay the 16 mm shaft data on top of the 18 mm data. Those two corrections are:

          • Multiply the measured 16 mm shaft velocity by (1+3.8%) so the oil flow rate through the valve is the same for both tests

          • At each shaft velocity, with the same oil flow rate through the valve, the damping force produced by the 16 mm shaft is going to be 3.8% greater due to the larger exposed valve area. So you have to multiply the 16 mm shaft force data by (1- 3.8%) to correct for the difference in valve face area

          That's the theory anyway, how does it work out?

           

          Corrected 18mm Shaft Rebound Data

          The dashed blue line in the plot below multiplies each 16 mm shaft velocity data point by (1+ 3.8%) to correct for the oil flow difference and multiplies the shaft force by (1- 3.8%) to correct for the valve face area difference. With those two corrections the 16 mm shaft data should theoretically overlay on the 18 mm rebound data.  

          The data does not line up. After correcting for oil flow difference and valve face area the 16 mm data (dashed blue line) still produces more damping forces than the 18 mm shaft. Looks like there was more going on in that test then a simple shaft diameter change.

           

          The reason for the difference is simple I think. When kyb changed to an 18 mm shaft they took that opportunity to increase the tuning range of the clicker circuit. When John Curea swapped shafts in the dyno test that also swapped the clicker bleed circuits built into each shaft. So the dyno test ended up with different clicker bleed circuits in each shaft – that difference is important to understand. Bike model-to-model and year-to-year clicker bleed circuits are not always the same. The data here shows and example of that.

          yz250 18 mm vs. 16 mm Shaft Dyno Data, Rebound

          There is no simple hand calc to correct the data for the differences in clicker flow area. Flow through the clicker circuit depends on pressure drop while flow through the valve only depends on shaft velocity. The flows are coupled, but in a non-linear way with no simple algebraic correction to relate the two. For non-linear coupled problems like that analysis tools make dyno data interpretation a whole lot easier.

           

          Put in the modified needle geometry and the computed damping force curves line up with the 16 mm and 18 mm shaft data. The 16 mm shaft produced more rebound damping force because of the larger exposed valve face area, larger swept oil volume and smaller clicker bleed circuit. We can double check the needle geometry difference by comparing the compression side data to see if the needle change lines up with that data as well.

          yz250 18 mm vs. 16 mm Shaft Dyno Data, Compression

          On the compression side things get more complicated. The 18 mm shaft pushes more fluid through the compression adjuster and that drives damping force up. But the larger shaft reduces oil flow through the mid-valve driving damping force down. Opposing effects make it hard to figure out if the damping force is going to go up or down with the larger shaft which is why russ17 asked the question in the first place.

          It would be cool to know how much damping force was generated by the compression adjuster and how much from the main piston. But dyno testing only measures overall shaft force so there is no way to tell out how much of the force was generated by each component. Regardless, russ17's question was what was the overall effect of the shaft change and the dyno measures that.  

          To get compression damping force out of analysis the bleed circuit force, compression adjuster and force from the main shock valve have to all be summed together. Sum up the components and the overall force turns out to be pretty close to the shaft force measured on the dyno. Dyno test results show the 16 mm shaft produced more damping force. But then again, the 16 mm shaft had the smaller clicker needle geometry so the difference in damping force shown by the data is a little bit exaggerated.

          Oil Foaming

          The other interesting thing in John Curea's dyno data in terms of understanding shock valving is the difference in hysteresis loops between the 16 mm and 18 mm shafts. The 18 mm shaft pushes more oil through the compression adjuster. That produces more back pressure on the shock chambers which in turn suppresses the hysteresis loop.  

          You can get a better grip on the “why” of that looking at the shock chamber pressures generated by the compression adjuster. At zero shaft velocity the oil reservoir starts out with a pressure of 150 psia. As the shaft accelerates, flow through the compression adjuster back pressures the shock driving pressures in the compression chamber up. That sets the value of P.c, the green curve. Meanwhile, the pressure drop across the main piston drives pressures in the rebound chamber down (P.r, the red curve).  

          Crank down on the compression adjuster and flow resistance through the compression adjuster goes up increasing the value of P.c which back pressures the entire shock and increases pressures in the rebound chamber (P.r). On-the-other-hand, put in a stiffer shock valve stack on the main piston and the pressure drop across the valve goes up driving pressures in the rebound chamber (P.r) down. Through tuning of the main valve compression stack and compression adjuster you can set pressures in the rebound chamber (P.r) to be any value you want.

           

          For the test here, the 16mm shaft rebound chamber pressures drop below the initial reservoir pressure and end up at a value of about 130 psia at a shaft velocity of 7 in/sec. As the shock is driven to higher shaft speeds back pressure from the compression adjuster drives chamber pressures back up so that the 16 mm shaft rebound chamber return to the initial reservoir pressure at shaft speeds around 15 in/sec.

           

          Chamber pressures with the 18 mm shaft (dashed red line) are different in an interesting way. The 18 mm shaft pushes more fluid through the compression adjuster. That produces more back pressure on the shock chambers so that the rebound chamber pressure never falls below the initial reservoir pressure. Keeping the rebound chamber pressurized over the shaft velocity ranges prevents the oil from foaming and that shows up in the dyno data John Curea measured for the shock in terms of a smaller hysteresis loop. In terms of shock valving basics that is an important concept for mx suspension tuning.

          Compression Adjuster Performance

          The effect of changing the shock shaft diameter shows up in the compression adjuster performance. The 16 mm shaft data (black line) has a larger hysteresis loop then the 18 mm data (red line). The larger 16 mm hysteresis loop creates a 9% change in damping force between the first half of the stroke and the second half. The data also shows a “hitch” in the curve above shafts speeds of 15 in/sec. That 15 in/sec shaft velocity marks the point where the compression adjuster has generated enough back pressure to return the rebound chamber pressure back to the original reservoir pressure (That was shown above in the chamber pressure plot).

          After the rebound chamber gets re-pressurized at 15 in/sec the oil “heals” itself collapsing the foam bubble. That causes the 16 mm damping force to jump up closing the hysteresis loop. That behavior doesn't show up in the 18 mm data since the chamber pressures never got low enough to foam out the oil. The right hand curves re-emphasize that point showing hardly any hysteresis loop for the 18 mm data after the data is corrected for changes in reservoir pressure over the stroke.

           

          Modeling shock performance with foamed out oil has some academic interest but frankly little practical use - Here's why: Sooner or later you will come up short landing a triple. When that happens high compression velocities when the rear wheel slams into the whoop face will foam out the oil in the compression stroke. That creates the situation shown in the above Roehrig video frame with foamed out oil below the piston. When the shock flips around into the rebound stroke the piston will just be winging back through foamed out oil producing no rebound damping at all. That launches you over the bars creating your basic snake bite suspension setup.

           

          How severely the oil foams out depends on both pressure and temperature. That means it is going to be different on the first lap compared to the last lap after the shock warms up. It's also going to be different at the start of a whoop field compared to the end as the foaming progressively builds. Inconsistent behavior that depends on the instantaneous oil temperature and stroke history just isn't worth modeling or trying to figure out how to tune.

           

          The fix is simple. Tune the compression adjuster to keep the rebound chamber pressurized at or above the oil reservoir pressure over the stroke velocity range. That keeps the gas dissolved in the shock oil from foaming out. Any time the pressure drops below the reservoir pressure all of the gas dissolved in the oil comes boiling out just like opening a hot bottle of coke. The 16 mm shaft data from John Curea shows that.

           

          While all of that sounds scary, it's important to understand data from that 16 mm shaft test isn't all that bad. The oil foamed out in the 7 in/sec range, but once shaft speeds cleared 15 in/sec the rebound chamber re-pressurized and the oil fixed itself. The shock was able to fix itself because the compression adjuster was set to be much stiffer than the mid-valve. So as shaft velocities increased back pressure from the compression adjuster eventually caught up with pressure drop across the main piston and re-pressurized all of the shock chambers. That fixed the compression stroke cavitation so the rebound side showed hardly any hysteresis at all.

           

          On-the-other-hand, hack around on the compression shim stack making it stiffer and you could easily overrun the tuning authority of the compression adjuster ending up in a situation where the chambers do not re-pressurize. When that happens the oil foams out and stays foamed out. Foamed out oil can create some really wacky behavior in a shock – Valving Logic shows an example of that.

          Along with a larger main piston when they made the change to the 16mm.

          A larger piston produces more damping force with a lower valve pressure drop.

          In that respect a larger piston is like switching to a larger shaft in terms of giving the compression adjuster more control over pressure balancing the shock.

           

          Penske Pressure Balancing

          Tuning the compression adjuster to keep the rebound chamber pressurized is a little different than what Penske describes for pressure balancing a shock. The Penske process, shown on the Roehrig site, emphasizes minimizing chamber pressures to reduce seal drag and enhance tire grip. The process recommended by Penske sets the compression adjuster so the pressure rise above the main piston matches the pressure drop behind the piston in the rebound chamber. The left hand figure shows what Penske recommends as the “Ideal balanced damper”.

          John Curea's dyno data for an '06 yz250 shock shows you need to move towards chamber pressures in the right hand curve “Excessive head valve damper”. That configuration keeps the rebound chamber pressurized and Curea's 18 mm shaft dyno data shows that suppresses oil foaming and the hysteresis loop measured in test. Back off on back pressure from the compression adjuster using the smaller oil flow of the 16 mm shaft and the hysteresis loop starts growing. Data from that test also shows the kyb shock used in those tests did not produce a bunch of seal drag causing the dyno force to hiccup every time the shaft changed direction through the zero velocity point.

          Valving Logic shows a second example of seal drag for a yz250 shock. Valving Logic was trying to diagnose the effect of an ultra stiff compression adjuster with nearly double the back pressure driving chamber pressures up into the 1181 psi range. Comparison of the stiff adjuster to a normal compression adjuster producing about 575 psi of back pressure showed the dynamic seal drag measured in test was about the same for both configurations. Seal drag for the kyb shock used in that test series was more-or-less independent of chamber pressure.

          There are two ways to pressurize the rebound chamber. One is crank up the reservoir gas pressure so that the drop in rebound chamber pressures over the stroke does not go low enough to foam out the oil (Penske approach in the left hand figure). The other approach is tune the compression adjuster to dynamically back pressure the shock as shaft velocities increase (right hand figure). The cool thing about dynamic back pressure is the chamber is only pressurized when shaft velocities are high. When shaft velocities go low (like changing direction through TDC) the compression adjuster does not produce much back pressure. That keeps chamber pressures low and potentially reduces seal drag. Create back pressure by cranking up the gas reservoir pressure and seal drags are higher everywhere.

           

          On-the-other-hand the Valving Logic data shows chamber pressures do not really effect seal drag. So the bigger effect for tuning the compression adjuster is setting the rate of dynamic back pressure to keep the shock chambers pressurized and suppress the hysteresis loop.

           

          There are different schools of thought in terms of what pressure balancing a shock means. Short stroke, high frequency applications of circle track racing may require different pressure balance criteria then shocks setup for the longer strokes and higher shaft speeds of off-road applications. The data here only looks at one shock configuration and that probably does not show the whole picture.

          Factory Connection High Speed Compression Spring

           

          You guys ever test the effects of a softer shock compression adjuster spring?FC sells them and wonder what they will do for dampening and cavitation

           

          Valving Logic tested a Factory Connection compression adjuster spring for an '11 kxf250 shock. To get an idea of what the FC spring does the first thing Valving Logic did was measure the stiffness of the spring:  

          • Stock: 0.78 kgf/mm

          • FC: 1.2 kgf/mm

          Some FC springs are stiffer – like the one tested here, others are softer. The FC kxf250 compression adjuster spring is 50% stiffer than stock.

           

          But what does that mean?

          The high speed compression adjuster spring pre-loads a plate (spring seat) that operates on the compression adjuster shim stack. Force from the spring acts on the edge of the shim stack clamp or reaches around to clamp some other shim in the stack (like the 10.2 shim in the drawing below). Either way, increasing pre-load on the spring makes the HSC stack stiffer.

          The stock kxf250 compression adjuster has 4 turns. At 4 turns in (1 mm/turn) the spring produces a force of 3.1 kgf. The stiffer FC spring produces that same force with 2 and 5/8 turns on the adjuster. So the FC spring gives an additional 1 3/8 turns of adjustment on the compression adjuster.

           

          But what does that mean?

          To figure out what the stiffer spring does in terms of damping performance Valving Logic ran a dyno test series on the compression adjuster. The test took all of the shims off of the main piston so the compression adjuster was the only active circuit in the shock. To make sense out of the data Valving Logic had to run a second test series measuring the drag force of fluid flow through the main piston ports. That drag force was subtracted from the compression adjuster data to get a true measure of compression adjuster force.

          At two turns out on the compression adjuster the dyno data shows the FC spring produces about 3 lbf more damping force then the stock compression adjuster.  

           

          But what does that mean?

          By itself the compression adjuster doesn't make much damping force. That surprises most people and doesn't line up with test ride results where a half turn on the compression adjuster makes a big difference in shock performance, way more than the 3 lbf shown in the above dyno data.

           

          The reason for that is the compression adjuster generates damping force in an indirect way. The compression adjuster back pressures the shock chambers and that prevents the main piston from cavitating. By suppressing cavitation the compression adjuster increases the damping force from the main piston– by a lot. Anyone who has ever cranked down on the compression adjuster could tell you that.

           

          To figure out compression adjuster performance we need to figure out how much back pressure the compression adjuster generates. To get a measure of that Valving Logic ran another dyno test series measuring the back pressure (Pc) produced by the compression adjuster for both the stock and FC spring.

          Compared to the stock compression adjuster the FC spring increases pressures in the compression chamber (Pc) by about 9 psi.

           

          But what does that mean?

          So now we're getting somewhere. The FC spring generates 9 psi more back pressure. With higher back pressure the main piston shim stack can be made stiffer to produce a 9 psi higher pressure drop without cavitating the shock chambers.

          In terms of performance:

          • How many face shims can you add to the main piston stack to get a 9 psi increase in pressure drop?

          • How much more compression damping force would that generate?

          Add one face shim to the stack and the valve pressure drop increases by 8.6 psi. That stiffer shim stack produces about 2.6% more compression damping force. So the FC compression adjuster spring allows the main piston shim stack to be re-tuned to produce 2.6% more damping force and still keep the chambers pressure balanced.

           

          But what does that mean?

          To figure out what a 2.6% more compression damping force means in terms of suspension setup we need some way to relate spring rate, mass and damping. Spring-mass-damper theory gives that relationship in terms of the “zeta” suspension response coefficient. Keep zeta the same and suspension response is the same – that's physics.  

           

          In terms of suspension response:

          • With 2.6% more compression damping how much could you increase the spring rate and keep damping performance (zeta) the same?

          • With the stiffer spring how much would the rider weight increase?

          Zeta controls both low speed and high speed damping: Low speed damping controls chassis oscillations and that depends on the weight of the chassis plus rider; High speed damping controls the wheels and that depends on the un-sprung wheel weight. Zeta dictates the response of both, the only difference is using the sprung or un-sprung weight in the Zeta equation.

          The compression adjuster is all about high speed damping of the un-sprung wheel weight. In evaluating zeta the wheel weight and linkage ratio are the same for both setups. That cancels out a bunch of stuff in the zeta equation. Hacking through the algebra shows high speed damping depends on a pretty simple relationship between spring rate (k) and the damping coefficient © squared.

           

          Increase high speed compression damping by 2.6% and the suspension is able to handle a spring rate increase of 5.3%. An '11 kxf250 stock suspension uses a spring rate of 5.2 kg/mm for a rider weight of 160 lb. Go over to the Race Tech spring rate calculator and a spring rate increase of 5.3% works out to a rider weight of 181 lbs.  

           

          Stiffer FC compression adjuster spring

          The stiffer FC compression adjuster spring allows you to add one face shim to the main piston stack and keep the shock pressure balanced. The stiffer damping allows the spring rate to be increase for a rider that weighs 21 lbs more. Go to a heaver rider than that and you are going to have to hack around on the compression adjuster shim stack to generate enough back pressure to handle the stiffer shim stack and spring rate of a heavier rider.

           

          To make that setup work the rebound damping needs to be increased to keep the rebound stroke zeta value is the same. Then the low speed damping needs to be increased in both circuits to handle the 21 lb increase in rider/chassis weight. Match zeta in those strokes and the suspension will bottom out, jump and damp like the stock setup with the stock rider weight – i.e. the setup the manufacturer intended for that bike. The only difference is matching zeta re-tunes the suspension for a rider that weighs 21 lbs more.
