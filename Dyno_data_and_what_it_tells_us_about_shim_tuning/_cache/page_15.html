          Suspension Response

          The “why” of that whole thing is easier to understand using the basic principles of spring-mass-damper theory shown on wikipedia.

          Theory shows there are only two parameters that effect suspension response: tau and zeta.

          Tau is the suspension response time. Tau sets how long it takes the rebound stroke to doong out to full extension. Tau is set by the ratio of mass to spring rate. Those same parameters also set race sag. Get race sag right and the value of tau on any 12” suspension is going to be the same.

           

          The second parameter is zeta. Zeta controls how the suspension baby buggies after a bump. Zeta depends on mass (m), spring rate (k), linkage ratio (LR) and damping coefficient ©. Chang any one of those and the value of zeta changes pushing the suspension into an under or over damped mode. Simple.

           

          For the kx250 link example above there were two steps:

          • Change spring rate to get race sag right. That set the value of tau to be the same for both link ratios
          • Change damping to get the same force at the wheel. That set the value of zeta to be the same

          With tau and zeta the same – the suspension response was the same and the rebound response was line-on-line for the two setups. Those two steps of setting tau and zeta are the basics of weight scaling. With tau and zeta the same the suspension response of a 120 lb, 250cc rider will be the same as a 220 lb, 450cc rider. Those weight scaled setups will bottom out on the same bump and baby-buggy into and out of a turn in the same way.

           

          There are a whole bunch of components in suspensions and each one of them can be tuned independently. But suspension response, and what you “feel” on a test ride, only depend on the actual forces produced at the wheel. Spring-mass-damper theory breaks that down into two simple parameters: tau and zeta. Set tau and zeta to be the same and suspension response is going to be the same regardless of weight, spring rate, damping or link ratio.

          Back to dyno data

          We started looking at “fo” crossovers and pulled up an example from MXScandinavia. That stack was dyno tested out to suspension wheel velocities of 360 in/sec and the crossover gap never moved. It was a “fo” gap.

           

          Valving Logic rmz250 Dyno Data

          Valving Logic shows another example of a “fo” gap in dyno testing of a rmz250 shock with a kyb 46mm valve. The maximum shaft velocity for this dyno test was 60 in/sec and did not close the crossover gap. Over the range tested that crossover gap was “fo”.

          But there is a catch. Push that stack out to higher speeds and the “fo” crossover gap closes. By analysis that occurs around a shaft speed to 140 in/sec (10.7 m/sec wheel speed). After the crossover gap closes the high speed stack comes into play making the stack stiffer reshaping the damping force curve into something a little different than shown by the low speed data.  

          The test here demonstrates one of those situations where you need to be really careful about how you go about interpreting dyno data. Over the range tested the data shows now evidence the crossover gap in that stack was closing. That leads to the conclusion the crossover gap was “fo”. In reality the crossover gap was active but there is no way the data is ever going to demonstrate that until the stack is pushed to higher speeds where the crossover gap actually closes.

           

          That brings up a separate category of “fo” crossovers. A crossover gap may be “fo” over the range tested, but when pushed to higher speeds on a test ride the crossover gap actually does close changing the damping force curve into something a little different than expected. That generates one of those classic situations where the dyno data just does not line up with the realities of a test ride.

           

          What makes a “fo” crossover gap:

          • Crossover shim diameters that are too large

          • High speed stacks that are too soft

          • Face shims that are too stiff

          The rmz250-54c stack above uses an interactive crossover that transfers force into the high speed stack. That causes the high speed stack to start deflecting before the crossover gap closes and because the high speed stack was soft it deflected at nearly the same rate as the face shims. All of that combined to push the crossover closure velocity out beyond the shaft velocity range that could be tested on the dyno.

           

          If the stack had used a smaller crossover shim, less force would be transferred through the interactive crossover into the high speed stack. That would reduce the high speed stack deflection and force the crossover to close. Likewise, a stiffer high speed stack would deflect less forcing the crossover gap to close earlier. Either one of those stack mods would alter the crossover gap behavior and the damping force curve.

          "fo" crossovers: A second and more confusing example:

          Crossovers usually close at low speed in the range of 10 to 30 in/sec. Pushing the closure velocity to higher speeds creates confusion in dyno testing especially when that shaft velocity is beyond the test range. Valving Logic shows an example testing a Showa 50mm valve for a rmz450 shock.

          In terms of shim factors, the low speed face shims on the rmz450-1415c stack (7x44.25 +44.2) are about 14% stiffer than the rmz450-1414c stack on the left. The rmz250-1415c configuration also uses a stiffer high speed stack. With stiffer face shims and a stiffer high speed stack it is pretty clear the stack on the right, 1415c, should be stiffer.

           

          Dyno testing showed the opposite result. The theoretically stiffer rmz450-1415c stack dyno tested to be softer and it was softer everywhere, both at low speed and at high speed. The dyno data show no indication that rmz450-1415c stack is ever going to catch up to the damping force measured for the theoretically softer rmz450-1414c stack.

          Why were shim factors and simple intuition so far off for those two stacks?

          The FEA stack deflection curves give some hints. The crossover gap on the 1415c stack closes at a stack force of around 200 lbs. The dyno test only produced about 140 lbf of fluid force on the stack at the maximum shaft velocity of 60 in/sec in the test. The crossover gap never closed, so the stiffer high speed stack in the 1415c configuration never came into play in the dyno test. In that sense the dyno test demonstrated the crossover gap was “fo”. But, run that stack at a higher speed and the crossover gap becomes active.

           

          Since the crossover gap did not close in the test, the only thing that mattered was the stiffness of the face shims. The face shims for the 1414c configuration had a tsf of 73 and the 1415c stack was at 77 by shim factors. So, the 1415c stack should have been stiffer but the dyno shows it was the other way around. What's going on there?

          The were two differences in the low speed stack configuration that shim factors did not account for in those stack configurations. The face shims on the 1414c stack were clamped on a 32mm shim in that split crossover. Face shims on the 1415c stack were clamped by a smaller 30mm crossover shim. The smaller crossover makes the effective clamp diameter on the face shims smaller and the face shims on the 1415c stack softer. Shim factors don't account for that, shim factors only use the clamp diameter at the top of the stack.

           

          A second difference: On the high speed stack: the 1414c configuration uses a 44mm diameter shim to start the high speed stack; the 1415c stack starts with a 42mm shim. That smaller diameter shim forces the low speed stack to deflect further to close the crossover gap. You can see the difference in face shim deflection needed to close the crossover gap in the stack deflection plots.

           

          The smaller diameter clamp on the face shims and increased deflection to close the crossover gap combine to make the 1415c stack softer at low speed. Test those same stacks at higher speeds and you can see the softer low speed damping of the 1415c configuration as well as the damping force kick up after the high speed stack comes into play. But to figure that out you have to see the high speed data. That is an example where you need the high speed data to give you the big picture of how the features in those stacks work and why the theoretically stiffer 1415c stack ended up dyno testing softer at low speed.

          DaveJ refers to that problem as “trying to understand a grizzly bear by looking at it through a straw”. With only the low speed data the damping force curves for those two stacks did not make much sense and didn't give any real clues why the theoretically stiffer 1415c stack dyno tested softer. Broaden the damping force view out to high speed and the effects of the stiffer high speed stack are apparent as well as the delayed closure of the crossover gap.

           

          Getting to "the why" of the above test results is a good example where the combination of theory, test and analysis are needed to fill in the gaps of dyno testing. Each brings a piece to the puzzle but you need to put them all together to get a broader view of the overall picture. Getting the stack deflection curves out of analysis gives some insight into how the various features of the shim stack create the effects observed in test. With that understanding you can go back and reconfigure the shim stack structure to exaggerate or eliminate those effects – and that is the basic principle of tuning.

          Crossover Tuning

          Dyno data from MXScandinavia, John Curea, Valving Logic and Kawamaha demonstrates use of four different crossover styles. 

          • Non-interactive: Face shims deflect independently

          • Interactive: Face shims transfer force into high speed stack before crossover gap closes

          • Trapped: Split crossover shim gets trapped in closing crossover gap

          • “fo”: Crossover does nothing and never closes

           

          Interactive and non-interactive crossovers defined by Jeremy Wilkey are the most common. Trapped crossovers and “fo” crossovers are two additional styles demonstrated in the dyno data on TT. Each style produces a slightly different behavior.

          Non-Interactive crossovers use a shim diameter smaller than the stack clamp. That allows the face shims to deflect independently from the rest of the stack up to the point where the crossover closes. After that the entire stack deflects. Non-Interactive crossovers are the configuration most people describe when talking about how a crossover works. 

          Interactive crossovers use a shim diameter larger than the stack clamp. The larger diameter allows face shims to transfer force through the crossover shim into the high speed stack before the crossover closes. The transferred force causes the high speed stack to start deflecting before the crossover closes. To close the crossover gap, the face shims have to chase down the moving high speed stack. That delays closure of the crossover and softens the crossover closure event.

           

          Interactive crossovers span a wide tuning range. Crossover diameters that are only slightly larger than the stack clamp basically behave the same as a non-interactive crossover. Installing progressively larger crossover shim diameters transfers progressively more force into the high speed stack delaying closure of the crossover gap. In the limit, enough force is transferred into the high speed stack causing the high speed stack to deflect at the same rate as the face shims so the crossover never closes. At that point the crossover is a “fo” gap and does nothing.

           

          The shock shaft velocity need to close an interactive crossover gap can be tricky to estimate. Larger crossover diameters increase force transfer into the high speed stack delaying closure of the crossover gap. On the other hand, stiffening the high speed stack reduces the high speed stack deflection forcing the crossover gap to close earlier. That makes the closure velocity a function of crossover diameter, gap height, high speed stack stiffness and stack clamp diameter. So it pretty much depends on everything and that makes it tricky.

          Many of the crossover shim stacks on TT spit the crossover into a pair of shims. Using a shim pair with staggered diameters softens the crossover shoulder and bend radius of the face shims. That helps to keep the faces shims from kinking on the sharp corner and makes the stack more robust.

           

          MXScandinavia shows splitting the crossover using a pair of shims with staggered diameters can create a situation where the larger diameter shim gets trapped in the closing crossover gap. When that happens the stack gets stiffer. That behavior can screw up dyno parametrics.

          “fo” crossovers are created by crossover shim diameters that are too large, or high speed stacks that are too soft. In either case the high speed stack deflects at the same rate as the low speed stack and the crossover gap never closes. When that happens the stack behaves basically the same as a simple tapered stack with no crossover at all.

           

          There is second class of “fo” crossovers. Dyno testing may not be able to produce the shaft velocity needed to close the crossover gap. The data makes the crossover look as if it were a “fo” gap. But when pushed to higher speeds under a test ride the crossover gap will close, bringing the high speed stack into play and creating a damping force curve much different than expected from the dyno tests. That situation emphasizes you have to be careful interpreting dyno data and trying to guess how the stack will perform when pushed to speeds beyond the range of the test data.

          Shim Stack Stiffness Measurements

          This thread started out looking at shim factors and stack stiffness measurements made by finger press and dyno backer limit tests. The MXScandinavia finger press data mechanically deflected the stack to get direct measurements of stack stiffness at extremely high stack lifts. That data worked out to be equivalent to suspension wheel velocities around 22 m/s, more than double the 9 m/s data obtained for that stack by conventional dyno testing. The finger press and dyno data both showed the same thing: Shim stacks designed to be equivalent by shim factors ended up with a 20% difference in actual stack stiffness.

          A finger press deflects the shim stack by a mechanical point load applied to the stack face. That is a little different than the fluid dynamic forces smoothly applied by oil flow over the valve port area. That difference creates uncertainty in the applicability of point load finger press data for use in shim stack tuning. To remove that uncertainty dyno tuners have developed other methods to measure shim stack lift directly in the shock through backer limit tests.

          Backer Limit Testing

          Backer limit tests simply put a stiff washer behind the shim stack and use the dyno to determine the shaft velocity where the shim stack hits the backer. That deflects the stack in a natural way using the actual oil forces in the shock and provides a direct measure of the shaft velocity needed to close the gap to the backer. No assumptions.

           

          MXScandinavia shows an example of backer limit tests in the ct2025/3025 dyno test series.

          Valving Logic used that same approach to estimate stack lift in kyb shocks for shim stacks developed for use on a rmz250.

          Crossover Closure Velocity:

          Valving Logic shows an example of a kyb 46mm shock for a rmz250 dyno tested with and without a crossover in the shim stack. The crossover softened low speed damping as expected. After the crossover closes both stacks have the same high speed stack stiffness. The FEA stack stiffness curves show the point where the crossover closes and the parallel lines after that point indicate both stacks had the same high speed stack stiffness. But the stack edge lift of the rmz250-249c stack was higher because of the crossover gap and that made the damping force softer at high speed.

          On the port edge lift curve it is easy to pick out where the crossover closes. That occurs with about 26 lbf applied to the stack and flow analysis for the valve shows that force occurs at a shock shaft velocity of 17 in/sec. Looking at the damping force curve there is no distinct event at 17 in/sec, or anywhere else in the curve, showing where the crossover gap closed.

           

          The dyno damping force curve clearly shows the rmz250-249c crossover stack (green line) was softer. The data also shows the slope of the damping force curve through the high speed range was the same for both stacks. But, there is no distinct event in the damping force curve showing where the crossover closes. The difference in stack stiffness before and after the crossover gap closes was just too small to produce any visible event in the damping force curve.

          Shim Factors

          MXScandinavia must have taken this class (LOL).

          Thickness cubed shim factors are a good rule of thumb and like you say it comes right out of beam theory. But once you get down to mixing and matching stacks shim factors just don't work out. MXScandinavia showed examples of that back at the top of this thread. Stacks that were supposed to be stiffer tested softer and visa-verse.

          Using that data I made a comparison of actual stack stiffness to shim factor stiffness (actually tapered shim factors). Tapered shim factors miss it by +/- 30%. Some times there close, other times not. For the MXScandinavia SF14 stack a 30% stiffness error is equivalent to adding 10 face shims. That just isn't close enough for seat-of-the-pants tuning. I think that was MXScandinavia's point.

           

          Probably worth mentioning this situation often gets reported the other-way-around. When using “beam theory” to replace a stack of face shims with thicker or thinner shims and the dyno shows the stacks are not equivalent many report that as a dyno measurement error. FEA analysis of the stacks MXScandinavia tested shows that was not the case. The stacks were in fact stiffer.

           

          Shim factors are better than nothing. But it is important to understand shim factor equivalent stacks aren’t.

          Stack Tuning

          So in your programme if I understand more now, you could take a rebound stack that's too fast at -1 click out and alter it theoretically so it is slowed down to get that same feeling at say -12 clicks out. So say I would add 2 face shims to see by trial n error for the same result.

          Either way looking at bringing the damping into a useable range.

          Do I understand the concept of your programme better now..?

           

          Lets try your example.

          From your description, test ride results showed you liked your rebound stack better at 1 click out. You are proposing to run a couple more test rides adding two face shims at a time until you get to a stack that lines up with the current stack run at 1 click out.

          Starting with MXScandinavia's SF14 stack and running it at 1 click out I get the orange curve below. That is the target stack. What happens when you run a test series adding two face shims at a time?

          1. First attempt we add two face shims (green line). Damping force is higher but short of target

          2. On the second attempt we add two more face shims. High speed damping kind of lines up out there beyond 40 in/sec. But the low speed damping is still too soft

          3. Third attempt we add two more face shims. Damping force lines up at 35 in/sec but low speed is still too soft

          Not sure where you would go from here based on your test ride results. Adding face shims increases damping force, but the low speed damping in Attempt #3 is still way short of target. High speed lines up, but low speed is too soft – and that difference in low speed damping is what you are going to feel most on a test ride. So would you add two more face shims to see how it turns out, or try something else?

           

          Looking at the progression of the first three attempts it is pretty clear adding more face shims is going to make the high speed too stiff and not give you the low speed damping you are looking for. That is the problem harrperf was talking about. Adding face shims makes the stack stiffer, but that target stack run at 1 click out basically has the bleed circuit shut off making the low speed stiffer. You just aren’t going to get there by making the shim stack stiffer.

           

          So lets try something else– higher viscosity oil.

          4. Change the 5wt shock oil for 10 wt. Got a little more low speed but not enough

          5. Change to 15 wt oil. Got more low speed but still need more to line up with the target stack

          6. Change to 20 wt oil. Got the low speed lined up, but now the high speed is too stiff. Need to run some more test rides modifying the stack again

          7. For attempt #7 pull off two face shims. High speed is better but still too stiff

          For attempt #8 pull off another two face shims. Now the high speed is pretty close and with 20wt oil in the shock we've got the low speed damping pretty close to the target stack.

          So after eight test rides adding face shims to the stack and then taking them off in favor of higher viscosity oil we've got something pretty close to the target damping force curve. Looks like the solution is 20 wt oil and two more face shims.

           

          Initially you were thinking you could just add more face shims to get the stack to line up with the one click target stack. After the initial three test rides you may, or may not, have been able to figure out adding face shims was not going to get you there. At any rate, working the problem we got to the approach of higher viscosity oil. That got the low speed damping lined up, but the high speed was too stiff. With just a couple more test rides taking off the added face shims we got to the target damping force curve.

           

          So it all worked out, but here are some questions:

          • After eight test rides do you still remember what the target damping force curve felt like?

          • Do your riders have enough patients to wait while you are hacking around trying different setups to figure out what works and what doesn't?

          The basic purpose of analysis, dyno testing and comparison of all of these “wavy lines” is figuring out what works and what doesn't. The exercise here was pretty simple: Match a one click stack. The initial thinking was all you needed to do was add face shims. After a couple of tests it became apparent that was not going to work and we needed to do something to stiffen up the bleed circuit. Changing oil viscosity worked, we could have also tried a clicker bleed stack. Making those changes through analysis or dyno testing gives a path where you can show up at the next track day with the right setup – or at least the one you thought you needed, instead of wasting another eight test rides trying to figure out how to get there.

           

          How long do you figure it would take to run eight test rides tuning this thing through seat-of-the-pants testing?

          Would seat-of-the-pants testing give you enough real world feedback to figure out adding face shims wasn't going to work?
