          MXScandinavia Straight v. Tapered Stack Dyno Test

           

          For the MXScandinavia test, Jake test rode the stacks while MXScandinavia dyno tested the stacks to measure the difference. Jake refers to the tapered stack as the “Normal stack” and the straight stack as the “Abnormal stack”. The Abnormal stack is basically a straight stack with a large stack of clamp shims.  

          Apply the tapered shim factor equation to those stacks and the straight stack should be about 4% stiffer than the tapered stack.

          Damping Force Curve Shape

          There are three shapes to a damping force curve. The shape refers to whether damping force increase more or less than a linear curve.

          • Linear: Damping force increases linearly with shaft speed (straight line)

          • Progressive: Damping force increases more than a linear curve (curve hooks upward)

          • Digressive: Damping force increases less than a linear curve (curves downward)

          The three curves above are simply the shaft velocity raised to some power (pow= 0.7, 1.0 and 1.3) and were setup so damping force matches at 20 in/sec. That makes the digressive curve produce more damping force at low speed and less damping force at high speed. The progressive curve does the opposite.

          Shocks are setup to produce a digressive damping force curve. The reason for that is the shock needs to produce heavy damping force at low speed for control of the chassis and light damping forces at high speed to allow the wheels to float over bumps.

           

          With that understanding of damping force curve shape the damping coefficient data for mog's stacks shows the shock was progressive from zero to 5 in/sec and then went digressive at higher shaft speeds.  

          The reason for the shift in curve shape is the shim stack is closed at zero velocity and all of the flow is force through the clickers. The fixed orifice flow circuit of the clickers makes the low speed curve progressive. Above 5 in/sec the shim stack cracks open and the damping force curves went digressive.

          Hack around on the shim stack configurations and you could make those curves above 5 in/sec progressive or even more digressive.

           

          If you followed that you can understand the discussion of curve shape can get really confusing. Shocks are going to start out with a progressive curve when on the bleed circuits and then transition to some other shape as the bleed circuits fight it out with the shim stack/valve port configuration.

          That is where plotting out the data as a damping coefficient really helps out to understand the curve shape as well as what is going on in the shock. If the damping coefficient increases with shaft speed the curve is progressive. If it decreases the curve is digressive. Simple.

          Straight Versus Tapered Stack MXScandinavia Dyno Testing

          MXScandinavia ran back to back dyno tests on a straight and tapered stack to measure the difference in the shape of the damping force curve. MXScandinavia dyno tested the stacks on a Ohlins four port valve while Jake test rode the stacks on a kx250 shock with a kyb 46 mm valve. From MXScandinavia:

          “this is for a Ohlins 46 mx damper and the piston is a 4-port wery similar to the kyb piston.”

          “wery similar”; what the heck does that mean? Click on Google translator and “wery similar: Scandinavian dyno term quantifying valve port geometries as similar, yet different.”

          I make a joke. The thing is, if you are looking for kx250 dyno data this is not it. Wrong valve. The port geometry is similar and the shape of the dyno damping force curve is similar as MXScandinavia says. But the magnitude of the damping force is different, so the dyno data from this test can not be compared to other kx250 data run on an actual kyb valve.

          Dyno Test Results

          By shim factors: the tapered stack has a shim factor of 238 and the straight stack 248. The straight stack should be about 4% stiffer. The MxScandinavia dyno data shows the two stacks tested out to give about the same damping force.  

          The biggest difference between the two stacks is right around 10 in/sec. At that point the tapered stack is slightly stiffer then the straight stack. That result is odd. Both stacks have the same number of face shims and the same 34.1/28.1 split crossover pair backing the face shims. With everything the same you'd think the stacks would have the same low speed damping, they don't. What the heck is going on there?

          Tapered stack stiffer at low speed

          The straight stack 34.1 mm transition shim has nothing backing it so the stack is clamped on the 28 mm shim. Pretty straight forward.

          On the tapered stack, the 34.1 mm transition shim gets trapped in the crossover gap as the gap closes. So the face shims start out with a 28 mm clamp, just like the straight stack, and transition to a 34 mm clamp as the crossover gap closes. The “trapped” 34.1 crossover makes the tapered stack get stiffer as the crossover closes. In this case the stack is about 8% stiffer at 10 in/sec.  

          As the dyno goes out to higher speeds beyond 10 in/sec the tapered stack gets softer and lines up again with the straight stack around 25 in/sec. What the heck is going on there?

          That just sounds backwards to me ?

           

          Yes, it's completely backwards.

          Normally a crossover would be softer at low speed and stiffer at high speed after the crossover gap closes. MXScandinavia demonstrates in the above test a crossover can actually be tuned to be stiffer at low speed through clever use of a “trapped” crossover configuration.

          The stack is only 8% stiffer so no big deal. On the other hand, if you where expecting the crossover to be ~8% softer and it tests out to be 8% stiffer then there is a 16% mystery there. MXScandinavia is showing the “why” of that which I thought was some interesting data.

           

          The more important point here is there is no inherent difference between the shape of the damping force curve for a straight or tapered stack. Both stacks produce a digressive damping force curve with nearly identical damping force values.

          High Speed Stack Stiffness

          Visually, you might expect the big chunk of shims in the tapered stack high speed section (the 38.25 through 28.25 shims) to be stiffer than the corresponding shims in the straight stack (6x28.25). They are not.  

          By shim factors the tapered stack high speed section (38.25 through 28.25) sum up to a shim factor of 94. The corresponding shims in the straight stack (6x28.25) sum up to a shim factor of 104. That makes the tapered section of the high speed stack 11% softer. So the tapered stack gets progressively softer as the stack is further deflected into the high speed range. That causes the tapered stack damping force to drop down and line up with the straight stack at high speed.

          MXScandinavia Straight v. Tapered Dyno Test

          The MXScandinavia straight versus tapered stack dyno test shows there is no dramatic difference in the shape of the damping force curve between the two stacks. In detail, the tapered stack is stiffer at low speed due to the trapped crossover and softer at high speed due to the softer high speed stack. Those differences are somewhere in the 8% range producing a small effect relative to the straight stack. Overall the damping force curve shape for the two stacks is nearly the same.

          As mog mentioned, a crossover stack is normally softer at low speed and then gets stiffer at high speed after the crossover gap closes. MXScandinavia demonstrates that is not always the case in this dyno test. The crossover stack here is actually stiffer at low speed due to the trapped crossover and softer at high speed due to the softer high speed stack. So there is no universal rule. More importantly, the dyno data demonstrate there is no inherent difference in the shape of the damping force curve between a straight or tapered stack. The MXScandinavia dyno data and FEA analysis both show that.

          Valving Logic Straight v. Tapered Stack Dyno Data

          The dyno data and FEA analysis both show the two stacks produce nearly identical damping force curves. That is very different than the 34% softer stack expected by shim factors. The data from this Valving Logic test is one of those examples where shim factors fail to estimate the stiffness differences between shim stacks giving results that don't match up with the reality of a test ride.

          The more important result and the reason we are looking at this test is the dyno data shows the damping force curve shape for a straight or tapered stack is nearly identical. Both stacks produce a digressive damping force curve with no visible difference in low or high speed damping. The Valving Logic data and MXScandinavia tests both show there is no dramatic damping force curve shape difference between a straight or tapered stack.

          Kawamaha Straight v. Tapered Stacks

          Kawamaha tested straight and tapered stacks using his hydraulic dyno on a yz250 kyb fork valve. Vietze posted those dyno results in a TT thread here. Kawamaha mentions in a different thread the thickness of kyb shims is actually 0.114 mm although he lists them as 0.10 mm. The stacks below list the shims as 0.10 mm because Kawamaha reports them that way, the FEA calculations use the actual shim thickness of 0.114 mm.

          Applying shim factors to the two stacks shows the tapered stack should be 59% stiffer than the straight stack.  

           

          Kawamaha Dyno Data

          By shim factors the tapered stack should be stiffer. The FEA analysis shows the same thing with the tapered stack 12% stiffer. The Kawamaha dyno data shows the opposite result with the tapered stack 12% softer. Results of the test are a riddle.   

          There is no way to figure out the why of that riddle with out more data testing the differences between these stacks. There is no other data on TT testing similar stack configurations so at the point the data simply is what it is.
