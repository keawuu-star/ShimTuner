Dyno gas force corrections

 There are two ways to compress a gas generally considered to bound the range of gas pressure changes over the stroke:

Constant Temperature Compression: When a gas gets compressed at constant temperature the reduction in volume causes the gas pressure to go up. DaveJ refers to that as Boyle's law, aka the ideal gas law
P2= P1*(V1/V2)

​Adiabatic Compression: When a gas is compressed in a thermally insulated chamber the compression causes the gas temperature to increase. That causes the pressure to increase because of the volume reduction and further increase because of the temperature rise. Thermodynamically the equation is:
P2= P1*(V1/V2)^gamma; where gamma= 1.38

 A big problem in dyno testing is both of those gas pressure extremes show up in dyno testing depending on the stroke speed. Vavling Logic demonstrated the effect in testing a PSF fork.

 [[IMG:8-vl-psf.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/8-vl-psf.png.e05893748755b44ddc19d444551b2f46.png]]

Constant Temperature Compression: Compress the fork slowly (0.1 in/sec – black curve) and the gas temperature increase from compression gets dissipated into the fork body. That keeps the gas at constant temperature so you end up with constant temperature (Boyle's Law) compression and a lower gas spring force

Adiabatic Compression: Compress the fork quickly (100 in/sec – red curve) and the 0.12 sec it takes a fork to blow through 12 inches of travel is not enough time for the gas to loose any heat. Fast compression with no heat loss creates an adiabatic compression and generates 175 lbf more gas force from the fork – demonstrated in dyno test

 Run the dyno at any intermediate stroke speed and you get wacky results where the gas looses some of it's compression heating but not all of it. That creates a gas spring force somewhere between the extremes of constant temperature and adiabatic compression with an uncertainty band of 175 lbf of gas spring force measured on the dyno.

PSF dyno testing

  

 Valving Logic shows an example at an intermediate stroke speed of 10 in/sec in the right hand figure below and compares that to the computed values (dashed line) of the Valving Logic PSF fork model (model downloaded from here). The fork starts off with 60 lbf of gas force at BDC. As the gas is compressed over the 8” compression stroke the fork ends up with 290 lbf of gas spring force at TDC. As the dyno rolls through TDC the gas looses heat to the fork body causing the gas pressure to drop so the fork starts the rebound stroke with a lower gas spring force of about 250 lbf. The compressed gas losses more heat through the first part of the rebound stroke producing a 50 lbf hysteresis loop where the gas force measured in rebound is lower than compression.

 [[IMG:9-psf-vel.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/9-psf-vel.png.1f8415a9e541d46cebb7793aca9ddb00.png]]

 The left hand figure shows how gas heat loss effects end up generating some wacky effects in dyno testing. At a stroke velocity of 3 in/sec (blue line) the gas looses heat as the dyno strokes through TDC causing the gas pressure and force to drop. As that cooled gas is expanded through the rebound stroke the gas force drops below the 0.1 in/sec constant temperature (Boyle's law) case. So constant temperature Boyle's law compression is not the lower bound of gas force – dyno testing shows actual gas force values drop below that level.

Open bath fork dyno testing

 The variation in gas spring force over the stroke due to heat loss effects causes big problems in dyno testing of open bath forks. The above example for a PSF fork exaggerates the situation but demonstrate the problem none the less. A fork generates about 10 lbf of compression damping at 20 in/sec. For a PSF fork the difference between slow and fast compression generates a 175 lbf change in gas spring force. The hysteresis loop created by heat loss adds another 50 lbf of uncertainty in the gas force. Trying to tease out a 10 lbf damping force measurement from dyno data dominated by gas force changes over the stroke that are an order of magnitude higher is extremely difficult. That makes it very difficult to get reliable damping force measurements out of open bath fork dyno testing.

  

 An example of just how far out of hand dyno gas force corrections can get is given by Kyle Tarry in the ReStackor thread. Kyle ran a dyno test series for himself on an open chamber fork. I'm sure Kyle understands compressing a fork drives the gas force up. But somehow he forgot all about that when he got the dyno data in hand and assumed the dyno measurements were direct measurements of damping force. With no gas force correction the dyno data on the compression stroke was a factor of two too high. On the rebound stroke, because gas force acts in the opposite direction of damping, Kyle's rebound data was way too low. Not because the dyno data was bad, but because Kyle Tarry did not understand how to correct the raw dyno measurements for the changes in gas force over the stroke. 

  

 Get a grip on the changes in gas force over the stroke and the dyno test uncertainties created by those gas force changes and you can appreciate the simplicity of the hydraulic dyno tests Kawamaha runs. Pumping hydraulic fluid through the valve gives a direct measure of damping force and completely removes the uncertainties created by gas force changes dyno testing has to deal with.

Closed chamber fork testing

 On closed chamber forks dyno tests use a different procedure. For closed chamber forks the cartridge can be taken out of the fork and mounted directly on the dyno for test. That completely removes the gas force uncertainty along with the uncertainties associated with drag of the stanchion tube seals and guide bushings. Direct damping force measurements from the cartridge of a closed chamber fork makes the dyno data a whole lot easier to figure out.

Why do gas force corrections matter?

 Sooner or later you will want to compare the suspension of the bike you are working on to some other bike that is setup well. You need to know three things to compare suspension response: weight, spring rate and damping. Those three values set the zeta coefficient, also known as % critical damping, used to compute the suspension response.

 [[IMG:10-spring-mass-damp.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/10-spring-mass-damp.png.6c4195233cfd2028e5437061c2b9625f.png]]

 Springs make a suspension oscillate up and down. Damping stops that oscillation. In that sense spring force is the opposite of damping. The zeta coefficient shows that effect. The damping coefficient (c.shock) is in the numerator. Increasing damping increases the value of zeta making the suspension more stable. The spring stiffness constant (k.spring) is in the denominator. Increasing the spring stiffness reduces the value of zeta making the suspension less stable.

  

 Dyno shaft force measurements have the gas force intermingled with the shock damping force. If you don't separate the gas spring force from damping you are going to mistake a high gas force as if it were damping. That is not just bad - it's bad squared - because the gas spring force is the opposite of damping. The dyno tests above show the gas force corrections are in the range of 20 to 30% at low speed and that makes a big difference in the interpreted suspension response.

Dyno test data accuracy

 Start poking around in dyno data and the uncertainties of shock absorber testing become frighteningly large.

A single flake of silt trapped in the shim stack changes damping force by 20%

Valve face dish variations of +/-0.0005” change damping force by 13%

Shim friction changes the effective stiffness of shim stacks by 17%

Configuration uncertainties, like the clicker needle geometry in John Curea's test, create unexpected test-to-test differences

PVP compared to CVP dyno measurements show low speed damping force differences of 20 to 30%

Changes in gas force from BDC to TDC create a 35% uncertainty in EMA low speed damping force measurements

Open bath fork dyno tests generate gas force changes over the stroke that are larger then the damping force the dyno is trying to measure

 Dig a little into the uncertainties of dyno testing and it is easy to convince yourself you could never obtain reliable damping force measurements. But, the above examples have only been looking at the extremes. How bad is it really in day-to-day testing?

Repeat dyno tests don't

 The simplest way to get a handle on dyno test accuracy is look at back-to-back tests. John Curea provides an example in testing the Dogger's crf450r shock setup. The first dyno test ran the modified rebound stack Curea recommended for the Dogger. The second test ran the Dogger's rebound setup. Both tests used the same compression shim stack. In theory the compression data from those two tests should be line-on-line.  

 [[IMG:11-dogger.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/11-dogger.png.32a88a8e518d462ac954dd9cf172e828.png]]

 Results of those back-to-back tests showed compression damping force measurements repeated within 3.8% at the peak shaft velocity. The bigger problem was at mid-stroke where the hysteresis loop created a 13% uncertainty window in compression damping force values.

  

 Repeatability within 3.8% is pretty good. On the other hand, the test was trying to demonstrate a 10% change in rebound damping. Since the second test went 3.8% low on compression it may have also been 3.8% low on rebound as well. So the 10% rebound damping force increase the test was trying to demonstrate may have only been 6.2%. That is a 38.0% uncertainty on the result.

Probability Distribution Function

  

 "There are three kinds of lies:

Lies

Damned lies

And statistics"    - Mark Twain

  

 To get some detail on error analysis we have to rely on lies of the third type “statistics” to create what is know as a probability distribution function. The probability distribution is created by examining each dyno measurement and asking the question “how far off is it”. If that data point is off by 3% you put that point in the 3% error bin. If it is off by 5% it goes in the 5% bin, and so on. At the end the number of data points in each bin are summed up to figure out how often measurements are off at each error level. 

  

 For the 56 dyno runs on TT the probability distribution looks like this:

 [[IMG:16-pdf.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/16-pdf.png.a6c6ff08b77382141d54d55368220af7.png]]

 The first thing to check out in the error distribution is how peaked the curve is at 0% error. If that curve were flat on top, say from -5% to +5% error, that would tell you the measurements lacked sensitivity. For example, if you tried to measure 1 lbf with a 2,000 lbf load cell the instrument may not be sensitive enough to measure those small force changes. In that case you would get random force measurements splattered over the +/- 5% error range and the PDF curve would be flat on top. The data here shows no evidence of that. The curve is nicely peaked around 0% error indicating the dyno force and shaft velocity measurements are sensitive enough to resolve small damping force differences and generate a nicely peaked Gaussian shaped curve.

  

 The second thing to look at is whether the data is skewed to one side or the other. If the error distribution had more data points on the left then the right that would tell you when the dyno went off it tended to always measure low. That might mean something like as the shock heated up in test the temperature increase caused the oil viscosity to thin out so the damping force measurements went low. If the data was skewed the other way with more data points on the right hand side that would mean the dyno tended to read high. That might indicate the gas reservoir pressure went up as the shock heated up in the test causing the measured damping force values to go high when the shock got hot.

  

 Error distributions that are skewed to one side or the other give you some clues to what might be going wrong in test causing the errors. And that is a good thing. If you knew the dyno tended to always read high or low you could try and figure out what is causing the error and change the test procedure or instrumentation to fix or at least minimize the measurement error.

  

 The data here shows no sign of being skewed to the left or the right. That is a bad thing in terms of the errors are just plain old random noise with no rhyme or reason why the measurements went high or low.

  

 Error range

 Figuring out the error range from statistical analysis becomes a big foggy mess. The usual method is pick some fraction of the data, the confidence level, and estimate the error range needed to bound that fraction of the data. The plot above shows the error range bounding 80% of the data.

  

 Bounding 80% of the data sounds pretty good but creates problems in dyno tuning. Suppose you wanted to increase damping force by 10%. To do that a series of dyno runs are made adding face shims and hacking around on the stack tapper to get to a 10% increase. If that takes five tests an 80% confidence interval means 4 out of 5 tests (80%) will be within the error range and one test in five (20%) will probably be outside the error bounds. Hopefully the last test, the one that demonstrated a 10% damping force increase, is not the one test in five that was outside the normal error bounds.

  

 Most of the data in the probability distribution are lumped around 0% error. So on any individual dyno run the most probable result is the data is good. Run five tests hacking around on the shock configuration and statistics will tell you 4 out of the 5 tests were probably good. But every once in awhile statistic also tells you the dyno is going to pull out some wacky value that is off in the 10 to 15% error range.

  

 That is the problem with statistical analysis, you just don't know for sure. The measurements are probably good. But one test in five is probably bad and every once in a while the dyno is going to pull out some wacky value out in the 10 to 15% error range. In the end, you just don't know for sure. That is what statistics shows you.

Shim Factors

 This thread started out looking at shim factors and dyno test of shim stacks designed to be equivalent by shim factors. Comparison of shim stack stiffness computed by FEA analysis to the stiffness estimated by shim factors showed differences in the range of 30%. Now that we have looked at a few more dyno tests how well do shim factors compare to the rest of the dyno test data?

 [[IMG:1-tsf-data2.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/1-tsf-data2.png.18ea2546e48a40d9caeed042a024d68a.png]]

 The first couple of dyno runs and the finger press data from MXScandinavia were used to set the calibration coefficient of 40 used in the above shim factor equation. That factor was set to bound the test data within an error range of +/- 30%. In terms of shim stack design an error range of +/-30% works out to be equivalent to adding +/- 10 face shims to the MXScandinavia SF40.2 stack.

  

 Adding the rest of the test data does nothing to improve shim factor accuracy. It just fills in the data scatter and reinforces that original error estimate of +/- 30%.

  

 Why are shim factors so far off?

 MXScandinavia demonstrated one reason. Tall stacks are stiffer than short stacks due to the Fulcrum, Stacked Bowl or Internal Strain theory, pick your favorite. It is not clear which theory is the cause, but the bottom line is tall stacks are stiffer and the shim factor equation does not include that effect.

  

 Crossover gaps are another reason. Stacks like rm85-750 and rmz250-875 from Valving Logic shown below have a crossover gap. Crossovers make the stacks dyno test softer then expected by the shim factors simply because shim factors do not account for crossover effects. Correcting shim factors for crossover effects gets tricky because there are active, non-interactive and "fo" forms of crossovers. Each of those effect stack stiffness in a different way. The "fo" gap of the Valving Logic rmz250-54c stack is an example. That stack has a crossover gap so you would expect the stack to dyno test on the low side along with all of the other crossover stacks. Instead that "fo" gap dyno tests on the high side running along the +30% border in the above plot.

 [[IMG:2-crossover-stacks.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/2-crossover-stacks.png.9889532114d3ddda3e379d709a715712.png]]

 Simple stacks with no crossover are another problem for shim factors. Stacks like the ohlinsMX-clmp3025 tested by MXScandinavia and the rmz250-152 stack from Valving logic are stiffer then expected by the shim factors. Those simple straight stack configurations produce stack stiffness that are above the +30% shim factor error bar on the plot above.

 [[IMG:3-straight-stacks.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/3-straight-stacks.png.9e14d8d46d5ace54f8ace68b25f0e4bc.png]]

 SA simple tapered stack configuration like the MXScandinavia Jake02 stack you would expect to more-or-less fall in with the other straight stack data. Instead that stack tested softer and falls down at the bottom of the plot with the crossover stacks below the -30% error bar line.

  

 Results of shim factor testing do not show any simple rule that determines when shim factors will under or over estimate the stiffness of a shim stack – at least I can't figure one out. The data shows the results are simply scattered over the +/- 30% error range. That 30% error range is equivalent to adding +/- 10 face shims and that just isn't a worthwhile estimate of shim stack stiffness when it comes down to the details of damping force tuning.

Reduce low speed damping

 Adding a crossover softens low speed damping. Valving logic demonstrated that effect dyno testing stacks with and without a crossover.

 [[IMG:4-crossover.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/4-crossover.png.e54bab62ae0666e84dcebe9219d55214.png]]

 Adding a crossover softens damping, that is not a big surprise. The problem is the crossover also softens high speed making the damping force softer everywhere. So to soften low speed damping you have to do two things:  

	
 Add a crossover to soften low speed damping
	

	
 Stiffen the shim stack to get high speed damping back to where is was
	

 Something like this:

Damping force curve shape tuning

 The above dyno tests demonstrate three basic damping force curve shapes and the shim stack configurations for each:  

	
 Simple tapered stack for a linear damping force curve
	

	
 Crossover shim stack to reduce low speed damping
	
	
	
 Ring shim preloaded stack to increase low speed damping
	

 [[IMG:8-shape-tuning.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/8-shape-tuning.png.5929838a1297006635dae5ac93d406c3.png]]

 The tricky thing for tuning is there is no independent control of low and high speed damping. If you want more low speed damping you have to do two things: preload the shim stack AND soften the shim stack. Otherwise the stack just gets stiffer everywhere. Likewise a crossover reduces low speed damping, but you have to do two things: add a crossover AND increase the shim stack stiffness to get high speed back in the ballpark. Otherwise the stack just gets softer everywhere.

  

 That makes dyno tuning on the test stand a PIA. If you want to increase low speed damping by 10% you have to add a ring shim to preload the stack and then hack around through a couple of dyno tests to get the right ring shim thickness and preload to match the 10% target. After that a second series of dyno tests is needed softening the stack to get the high speed back to where it was. At the end, that softer stack needs a couple more dyno tests hacking around on the thickness of the ring shim to get low speed damping back to the 10% target. That may end up taking 7 to 10 dyno tests and gets expensive at $50 a stroke on the dyno.

Damping force shape tuning secrets

 Shim stack preload and crossover gaps give an unlimited tuning range once you understand how to use them. In the limit a crossover can have a single face shim and produce basically zero low speed damping. Changing the gap height controls when the high speed stack kicks in and tuning the high speed stack stiffness controls how stiff that is going to be. Alternatively large preload on a stiff shim stack will hold the shim stack closed, hydraulically lock the shock, making the shock infinitely stiff.

  

 Between those limits of zero and infinity there is a damping force curve that is going to work. But you have to know how to manipulate the shim stack to get those curves. That gets a little complicated because changing the damping force curve shape requires simultaneous changes to the crossover gap or stack preload AND the stack stiffness. You can't get there only changing one thing at a time in the usual tuning approach.

I'm looking at working on a preload stack to get low speed,bleed shim to get rid of ugly spike before shim stacks open with a soft single stage for less hs

  

 The only reason to preload a shim stack is to increase low speed damping. If that ends up creating too much low speed you either have a shim stack that is too stiff or too much ring shim preload.

  

 This preload discussion got started by MidlifeCrisisGuy looking for a way to soften high speed damping for square edge bump compliance. The problem he and 2grimjim had is they could not figure out how to soften high speed and keep enough low speed damping to prevent the chassis from wallowing around. Their solution was disarm the compression adjuster, imo cavitation from that mod causes other problems.

  

 It's easy to understand their frustration:

 The stock stack had a crossover. By conventional thinking the crossover should soften low speed and get stiffer at high speed after the crossover closes

 To get something with the same low speed and less high speed the fix should be a straight stack. With nothing but face shims a straight stack should be as stiff as possible at low speed. Using a straight stack and backing off on the stiffness should give less high speed damping and the maximum possible low speed

 Something like this: Starting with the rmz450-1415c stack tested by Valving Logic.

 [[IMG:9-straight-stack.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/9-straight-stack.png.6122eea67c0c3975f1d11995ad078886.png]]

 The above example shows the straight stack ends up with less low speed damping than the original crossover stack. Those results imply there is no way to back off high speed without loosing low speed damping. So you are stuck.

  

 Add a ring shim and things are different. With preload from the ring shim a softer stack can match the low speed damping of a stiffer stack and get softer at high speed simply because the stack is softer. For example:

 [[IMG:10-ring-shim.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/10-ring-shim.png.974a8d254599d1dd96a5750629ea2224.png]]

 Getting that to work takes some tuning:

 Too much preload and you are going to end up with an ugly low speed damping force spike

 Too stiff of a stack and there is going to be an ugly damping force spike at low speed and too much damping at high speed because the stack is stiffer

 To get something that works it's got to be the right stack stiffness with the right preload. Mess up either one of those and it is not going to work. You can soften low speed damping a little bit with clicker adjustments or a valve port leak jet if you have to. But, either one of those mods is going to muck with rebound damping. So you would have to go fiddle with the rebound stack to make it a little stiffer, compensating for the added bleed, to get rebound back to where it was.

  

 The only reason to put a ring shim in the stack is to increase low speed damping. If that ends up producing too much low speed damping and an ugly low speed spike you either have a shim stack that is too stiff or a ring shim with too much preload. You need to back off on one or the other – no reason it has to be ugly.

 Shim Stack Tuning Webology:

 So far, this thread has looked at six points of shim stack tuning webology. Some theories have worked out, others have not. Here is a summary of webology theories verified with dyno data so far:  

 Shims become stiffer at edge lifts greater than one shim thickness

 False: The MXScandinavia finger press data shows shim stiffness is nearly linear out to edge lifts of 7 shim thicknesses. For a shock that is equivalent to a wheel speed of 38 m/sec and well beyond the 7 to 10 m/sec speeds generally considered to be the extremes of MX suspension velocities. If you can not see any effect at 38 m/sec it just is not going to show up in a suspension system

 Shim stiffness becomes nonlinear at high lift

 False: The MXScandinavia finger press data shows shim stiffness is nearly linear out to edge lifts of 7 shim thicknesses. Data from that test is equivalent to hitting a 7 inch bump at 140 mph 

 A second point here: the data shows shim stiffness is “nearly” linear. It is not “exactly” linear, but far close to linear than the strong non-linear effects commonly expected based on Belleville springs

 A tall stack of thin shims is stiffer than a short stack of thick shims

 False: The stacked bowl theory and fulcrum theory both assert tall stacks are stiffer. Those theories made sense against the first two MXScandinavia dyno tests modifying the low and high speed stack. Further tests changing the height of the high speed stack did not support the theory. So “sometimes” tall stacks are stiffer, sometimes they are not. Rules that only work sometimes just aren't very useful for suspension tuning

 A tall stack of thin shims is more progressive than a short stack of thick shims

 True: The stacked bowl theory asserts shims added to the top of the stack are force to bend around a sharper bend radius. That theory supports the dyno data showing tall stacks of thin shims are stiffer, demonstrated by the SF4/SF14 finger press data comparison

Stiffer high speed stacks increase high speed damping
False: Increasing the stiffness of the high speed stack makes the damping stiffer everywhere, not just at high speed. The Ohlins dyno data, the MXScandinavia dyno data and the FEA analysis all show that effect

 A straight stack is stiffer than a tapered stack

 False: Shim stacks can be straight, tapered or an upside down Christmas tree. All that matters is the stiffness the stack structure produces at the face shim. The Valving Logic data and MXScandinavia data both demonstrate there is no difference in the shape of the damping force curve between a straight or tapered stack

 There may be other dyno data out there showing some of these webology theories work. The dyno data here shows they do not work. Theories that only work sometimes just aren't very useful for suspension tuning.

Why dont we just back it all up to basics.

 The idea of suspension is to take the energy produced from an event such as a bump or jump and neutralize that energy through pressure that is generated by the restriction of oil to flow  mainly.

 . Ive been making my own pistons and doing some data logging and I feel that it gets made to be more complicated than it really is. &%$#@! the shims and just think about a shock piston with no shims for a minute. This is still speed sensitive isn't it? the faster you move the piston (with no shims) through the oil the the more Dampening. Why do you ask? Because no matter how fast the shock shaft moves the the amount of oil that needs to be displaced is relative to the amount of stroke used. If we use 100mm of travel at slow speed we have more time to displace the oil. This means that the oil flows through the holes in the piston with less velocity and in return generates less pressure in the shock. If we doubled the speed of the shaft we need to displace the same amount of oil in half the time. This means the oil will have to move through the port at twice the velocity and in return generate even more pressure in the shock, it is the pressure that dampens the shock movement. Low speed, high speed we have become obsessed with these terms. Shims don't know the difference they open as far as they can relative to their stiffness and the pressure of the shock. If you have less shims on a piston the shock will accelerate faster (this is most people idea of plush, Bad Bad Bad) in turn the shaft will reach a higher velocity and it turn become harsher quicker.
