The crossover stack here is actually stiffer at low speed due to the trapped crossover and softer at high speed due to the softer high speed stack. So there is no universal rule. Why do factories use a tapered stack?

 Manufacturing wise, tapered stacks are a PIA. It is a whole lot easier to buy, stock and assemble straight shim stacks using a single shim diameter. Why do manufactures use tapered stacks?

 Some webology theories:

 The stack has to have a Christmas tree shape to keep the stack from hitting the backer

 You have to taper the stack to soften high speed damping

 The stack has to have a series of large diameter face shims to stiffen low speed damping

 The stack needs to have a range of shim diameters so it can be fine tuned

 Here is another theory: Robustness and Reliability

 On a straight stack all of the shims are forced to bend at the clamp, there is no other pivot. At high deflection the single pivot point at the clamp forces a sharp bend creating a stress concentration point. The sharp bend can push shims beyond the yield point. So, the first time you pile your bike into a whoop face the large blow permanently bends all of the shims at the clamp. Then you have to replace the entire stack. That is time consuming, expensive, and a PIA that gives manufactures a bad rep.

 [[IMG:13-clamp-stress.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/13-clamp-stress.png.367d256765d91992989df275a7ae07b3.png]]

 A tapered stack spreads out the clamp force. The face shims on a tapered stack bend around a larger radius with a smooth arc like shape. At high deflection there is no kink at the clamp creating a stress concentration point. That allows a tapered stack to deflect to higher edge lifts without stressing the shims beyond the yield point. Spreading out the clamp force makes tapered stacks more robust against unexpected large hits. Simple and robust.

 MXScandinavia pointed out that the stacks in older KTM forks had a problem with over stressing shims at the clamp causing the shims to become permanently bent. Re-tuning the stack with a larger taper fixes that.

  

 Just a guess - I think the OEM's use taper for high frequency fatigue life of the shim.
 

 Whether it's high cycle fatigue; low cycle fatigue; tempering or material defects: A tapered stack spreads out the clamp force and reduces stress on the shim. That makes the stack and the shock more robust against unexpected large hits and is the basic reason why shim stacks are tapered.

 Is a straight stack stiffer than a tapered stack?

 First off, what does that question even mean? Like if a stack is stiffer, then it's stiffer. Duh!

Shim Stack Tuning Webology:

 So far, this thread has looked at six points of shim stack tuning webology. Some theories have worked out, others have not. Here is a summary of webology theories verified with dyno data so far:  

 Shims become stiffer at edge lifts greater than one shim thickness

 False: The MXScandinavia finger press data shows shim stiffness is nearly linear out to edge lifts of 7 shim thicknesses. For a shock that is equivalent to a wheel speed of 38 m/sec and well beyond the 7 to 10 m/sec speeds generally considered to be the extremes of MX suspension velocities. If you can not see any effect at 38 m/sec it just is not going to show up in a suspension system

 Shim stiffness becomes nonlinear at high lift

 False: The MXScandinavia finger press data shows shim stiffness is nearly linear out to edge lifts of 7 shim thicknesses. Data from that test is equivalent to hitting a 7 inch bump at 140 mph 

 A second point here: the data shows shim stiffness is “nearly” linear. It is not “exactly” linear, but far close to linear than the strong non-linear effects commonly expected based on Belleville springs

 A tall stack of thin shims is stiffer than a short stack of thick shims

 False: The stacked bowl theory and fulcrum theory both assert tall stacks are stiffer. Those theories made sense against the first two MXScandinavia dyno tests modifying the low and high speed stack. Further tests changing the height of the high speed stack did not support the theory. So “sometimes” tall stacks are stiffer, sometimes they are not. Rules that only work sometimes just aren't very useful for suspension tuning

 A tall stack of thin shims is more progressive than a short stack of thick shims

 True: The stacked bowl theory asserts shims added to the top of the stack are force to bend around a sharper bend radius. That theory supports the dyno data showing tall stacks of thin shims are stiffer, demonstrated by the SF4/SF14 finger press data comparison

Stiffer high speed stacks increase high speed damping
False: Increasing the stiffness of the high speed stack makes the damping stiffer everywhere, not just at high speed. The Ohlins dyno data, the MXScandinavia dyno data and the FEA analysis all show that effect

 A straight stack is stiffer than a tapered stack

 False: Shim stacks can be straight, tapered or an upside down Christmas tree. All that matters is the stiffness the stack structure produces at the face shim. The Valving Logic data and MXScandinavia data both demonstrate there is no difference in the shape of the damping force curve between a straight or tapered stack

 There may be other dyno data out there showing some of these webology theories work. The dyno data here shows they do not work. Theories that only work sometimes just aren't very useful for suspension tuning.

Crossover Tuning:

 How much does a crossover change the damping force curve? Valving Logic set out to answer that question by adding a crossover to a simple tapered shim stack. The baseline stack, rmz250-248c, uses a simple tapered shim stack on a kyb 46 mm shock valve. The second test, rmz250-249c, adds a single 24.15 crossover behind the face shims to see how much the damping force curve changes.

 [[IMG:1-crossover.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/1-crossover.png.fdcddc66844b4196a97fc8b82528bc73.png]]

 The crossover gap allows the face shims to crack open and deflect independently from the rest of the stack. The port edge lift curve computed above demonstrates the effect. From zero to 30 lbf on the stack the crossover stack (green curve) deflects more. Higher edge deflection softens low speed damping for the crossover stack.

  

 After the crossover gap closes the face shims are in contact with the high speed stack. Further deflection of the stack now requires the entire stack to deflect. That makes the stack stiffer and gives the crossover stack the same stiffness as the baseline stack at high speed. The parallel lines for the two stacks at high speed, above 30 lbf, means the stiffness is the same. Same slope, means they have the same stiffness.

  

 While the stiffness is the same at high speed, the flow area of the crossover stack is larger because the face shims are deflected across the crossover gap. That gives the crossover stack more flow area and softer damping. So while the stiffness of the two shim stacks are the same at high speed, the larger flow area of the crossover stack makes the crossover stack softer.

  

 The dyno results from this test show adding a crossover gap makes low speed damping softer. That is a very important tuning tool. Exploiting that capability has created many different crossover styles with subtle differences in the crossover geometry effecting shim stack stiffness and the way the stack transitions from low speed to high speed damping.

  

 TT has a bunch of dyno data demonstrating performance of different crossover styles.

Valve Geometries:

 About half of the valves used in the dyno test on TT are “free flowing” (rebound ports) and the other half are “non-free flowing”. The “non-free flowing” geometries are the compression side of the valve. So there is a bunch of data here for both "free flowing" and "non-free flowing" types valves.

 [[IMG:b1-TT-dyno-valves.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b1-TT-dyno-valves.png.fcb0bf2795786398656ce1c49b1b5f05.png]]

  

 Valve Port Geometry:

 [[IMG:b2-kyb46mm-rebound.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b2-kyb46mm-rebound.png.b0b5280bc524076e28399672f5fa1276.png]]

 The parameters r.port, d.port and w.port define the spill  paths out of the valve. Those parameters also define the valve pocket area, the pressurized surface on the shim stack face and the forces deflecting the shim stack.

  

 On the compression side the same parameters (r.port, d.port and w.port) define the dimensions of the pressurized valve pocket area. Compression valves usually also have a  valve port flow restriction defined by d.thrt. The port restriction produces a fluid jet acting on the outer perimeter of the shim stack to create the effects bbatarelo was talking about above.

 [[IMG:b3-kyb46mm-compression.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b3-kyb46mm-compression.png.9547045134d2c98771190710e2ba32a7.png]]

 On a large hit all of the flow through the valve is forced to flow through the d.thrt flow restriction. That causes the valve port to hydraulically lock up at high speed. imo, the primary reason manufactures put flow restrictions on compression pistons is to keep the shock from blowing through it’s stroke on a large hit.

  

 Entrance Flow Restriction:

 Another flow restriction you will see on suspension valves is a restriction at the port entrance. The entrance flow area is defined by h.deck measured between the valve deck and first shim on the reverse flow shim stack. That deck height area is often smaller than the valve throat and limits the flow at high speed.

  

 KTM fork valves are built with a low deck height for that reason. Delta shims are used on those valves  to space the shim stack off of the valve face and open up the flow area. Imo, that is a better approach then  a valve port flow restriction because the deck height is easily tunable by changing the delta shims. To retune a valve port flow restriction you have to fill up the valve port with JB weld and then re-drill a smaller throat.

 [[IMG:b4-hdeck.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b4-hdeck.png.3298e1d447e2b474a7fb3a2fd924997f.png]]

  

  

  

 [[IMG:b5-bbatarelo-valves.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b5-bbatarelo-valves.png.b5fa7299b4b8f3be10725d2fb29b584b.png]]

  

 For the valves bbatarelo shows the definitions of the r.port, d.port and w.port look pretty straight forward to me. For d.thrt, the port web on the Race Tech valve looks like something done for manufacturing convenience more then control of port flow area. There does not seem to be enough port web thickness in there to worry about. To get the calculations to ignore d.thrt set it to zero.

  

 Coming at this the other way, the Race Tech valve looks to have 100 times the port flow area of that Showa valve. So the Race Tech valve is not going to hit any flow limitations until suspension velocities pretty far beyond what Showa was thinking about for that shock application.

  

 Those valves all look pretty much the same with the exception of the showa HMAS valve. That valve is running a large seat width around the port.

 [[IMG:b6-showa-valve.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b6-showa-valve.png.c9eca58381fd85d67f14c6c8d86e4c3a.png]]

 At low speed when the shim stack is barely cracked open, the flow is forced through a very small slit between the valve seat and shim stack. When the valve has a wide seat, like the one above, that creates a large surface area and high viscous losses. Those viscous losses increase low speed damping. As the shim stack lifts further the flow area increases and the viscous losses go away.

  

 Fox used this same approach on their new RE:aktiv mountain bike shock. A large seat width and increased low speed damping was used to keep the bike from bobbing on pedal strokes.

One Thing At A Time

 The usual thinking for tuning is only change one thing at a time. Test ride the bike, see what it “feels” like and make corrections before things get too far out of hand. That makes sense.

 [[IMG:1a-one-thing.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/1a-one-thing.png.8fdd53889b0f18ccd4dfe6e50a9e866f.png]]

 That is a problem for crossover tuning. Put a crossover in the stack and the damping force changes everywhere, both a low speed and at high speed. Test ride the above setup and the dyno shows the damping force is flat too soft everywhere.

  

 To get to “one thing at a time” changes in the damping force curve you have to add a bunch of shims to the stack tapered section to get the high speed damping back into the range of the original stack. For the stack tested above that requires doubling every shim in the stack taper.  

 [[IMG:1b-one-thing-damping.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/1b-one-thing-damping.png.a0ef7e7ac56706aeea0d798ca646c068.png]]

 So here is the dilemma for crossover tuning:  

 Change one thing at a time in the shim stack and everything in the damping force curve changes. Test ride that and the entire damping force curve is mucked up

 To get to “one thing at a time” changes in the damping force curve you have to make a bunch of changes to the shim stack so that only one part of the damping force curve gets changed at a time

 Dyno's were invented for that very reason. With a dyno you can add or remove a crossover and then hack around on the rest of the shim stack so that only one part of the damping force curve gets changed. Test ride that and you can “feel” if the change in the damping force curve went in the right direction and fine tune it. That keeps you from wasting a bunch of time test riding wacky shim stacks that are way off the mark.

  

 Tuning “one thing at a time” makes sense. The problem is getting to a “one thing at a time” change in the damping force curve ends up requiring a bunch of simultaneous changes to the shim stack – and that gets tricky.

Non-Interactive Crossovers:

 Jeremy Wilkey on Dirt Rider defines Independent and Non-independent as the two basic styles of crossovers. I refer to those as Non-Interactive and Interactive. Same thing, different lingo.

  

 Non-interactive crossovers are the configurations tuners talk about when describing how a crossover effects the damping force of a shock. Non-interactive crossovers use a shim diameter that is smaller than the stack clamp. That allows the face shims to deflect independently from the rest of the stack up to the point where the crossover gap closes. After that the entire stack deflects making the stack stiffer at high speed. The operation of a non-interactive crossover is pretty straight forward.

  

 John Curea's dyno test of the Dogger's crf450r rebound stack demonstrates operation of a non-interactive crossover. The dyno test was run on a Showa 50 oval shock valve.

Interactive Crossovers:

 Jeremy Wilkey defines the second type of crossover as an interactive crossover. Interactive crossovers use a shim diameter that is larger than the stack clamp. When the crossover is larger than the clamp some of the force on the face shims is transferred directly through the crossover shim into the high speed stack. The transferred force causes the high speed stack to start deflecting before the crossover gap closes. Mog's stack dyno tested by MXScandinavia on a four port Ohlins valve is an example of an interactive crossover.

 [[IMG:4-interactive-co.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/4-interactive-co.png.088d752453c19580dc90a9a21294a781.png]]

 To close the crossover gap the face shims have to “chase down” the moving/deflecting high speed stack on an interactive crossover. That delays closure of the crossover gap and spreads out the crossover closure “event” over a range of shaft velocities. Mog's stack shows those effects. There is no kink in the stack deflection curve when the crossover gap closes and no kink in the damping force curve measured on the dyno for that stack.

 [[IMG:5-mog01.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/5-mog01.png.c49b9dc00c899feb13b06a83fe138d86.png]]

 Interactive crossovers have no distinct “event” when the crossover gap closes. Instead, the process is spread out over a range of shaft velocities. That introduces the idea of “mid-speed” damping where the damping force is somewhere in between the low speed force, defined by the face shims, and high speed force defined by the stiffness of the entire stack.

  

 Why do we see so few of these (non-interactive crossovers) types of stacks ?they seem better focused at the individual stages of ls and hs , I've only seem one oem with this a 04 rm

  

 Why so few non-interactive crossovers?

 Non-interactive crossovers have a well defined low speed and high speed stack separated by the crossover gap. That makes a lot of tuning sense, but creates a sharp transition when the crossover closes. Jeremy Wilkey points out that creates a shock that works “well” on one series of bumps but on the next series of slightly larger bumps the crossover closes and the shock suddenly gets too stiff.  

  

 The fix is an interactive crossover to spread out the transition and produce a more consistent feel across the shaft velocity range. That's the theory – in practice tuning gets tricky.

 Above, Jeremy Wilkey defined two basic types of crossovers: Interactive and non-interactive. Beyond those basic configurations the dyno data on TT demonstrates use of two additional types of crossovers: Trapped crossovers and “fo” crossovers.

  

 Trapped Crossovers:

 Instead of a single shim, trapped crossovers use a pair of shims with different diameters to form the crossover gap. As the crossover closes the larger shim gets “trapped” in the crossover gap. That causes the face shims to start out with a small clamp diameter and shift to a larger clamp when the second shim gets trapped in the closing crossover gap. The change in clamp diameter causes the low speed damping start out soft and transition to a stiffer rate as the crossover closes.

 MXScandinavia dyno testing of the Jake Normal stack demonstrates use of a trapped crossover.

"fo" Crossovers:

 The fourth type of crossover is a “fo” crossover. “fo” crossovers, like “fo” fur, are fake and create a crossover gap that never closes. “fo” crossovers are generated by crossover shim diameters that are too large or high speed stacks that are too soft. When the crossover diameter is too large too much force is transferred through the crossover into the high speed stack. That forces the high speed stack to deflect at the same rate as the face shims so the “fo” gap never closes. “fo” crossovers do nothing other than cause confusion. MXScandinavia shows some examples.

 [[IMG:7-fo-mx2c.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/7-fo-mx2c.png.f7121c003ae6f4f90b662f4c766c5365.png]]

 “fo” gaps are sensitive to changes in the shim stack configuration. For example on the above stack: If you reduce the crossover shim diameter a tad, less force is transferred through the crossover into the high speed stack. That reduces the high speed stack deflection and converts what was a “fo” crossover into a regular interactive crossover.

 [[IMG:8-fo-fix.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/8-fo-fix.png.f31ae01ec318208bcb975dea8adfaac6.png]]

 Crossovers that flip into and out of a “fo” configuration drive dyno tuners crazy. With only minor changes to the stack taper or clamp a crossover can flop over into a “fo” configuration causing the whole damping force curve to flop over into a different shape – often by a bunch.

 Those sudden shifts in the stack behavior lead to all kinds of wacky dyno based conclusions like non-linear shim forces or complex three dimensional fluid dynamics dominating the shocks performance. Worse, it prevents you from getting the damping profile you wanted, not because the shim stack was bad, but because things were going on in the stack nobody understood when dyno testing the shock. “fo” crossovers flipping into and out of an active configuration can wasting weeks of dyno test time trying to figure out what is going on in the stack.

Non-Interactive Crossover Configurations

 The dyno data on TT shows some of examples of non-interactive crossovers. We'll take a look at a few here and more latter comparing the effects of different crossover styles.  

  

 crf450r Dogger rebound

 John Curea, using the Mx Tech dyno, ran a couple of dyno tests helping the Dogger setup his crf450r rebound stack on the Showa 50 oval port valve used in that shock. The Dogger's rebound stack had a conventional non-interactive crossover with a shim diameter smaller than the stack clamp. The configuration allows the face shims to deflect independently from the high speed stack. The stack deflection curve shows the behavior you would expect for a non-interactive crossover: Soft at low speed until the crossover closes and a sudden transition to a stiffer rate after that point.  

 [[IMG:9-dogger-rbnd.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/9-dogger-rbnd.png.9d4384317cb6b5af121a7d495f28c2ab.png]]

 Normally, the stack face shims hold the valve port closed at low speed. When the valve port is closed all of the flow is forced through the clicker bleed circuits driving low speed damping forces up. But, the Dogger's stack only had four face shims. The soft face shims allowed the valve port to blow open at low speed so the flow bypassed the clicker bleed circuits. The end result is the stack produced little low speed damping making a damping force curve that was nearly linear all the way down to a shaft speed of zero.

So is this desirable ?a linear force curve ?

  

 No, a linear damping curve is not desirable. Agree with tmsuspension.

  

 Here's the theory:

 250 lb bike, 180 lb rider, 40/60 weight split. Take off 50 lbs for the front wheel and another 50 for the rear and the rear chassis weight is around 200 lbs. At 200 lbs the chassis needs some stiff damping for motion control, but at the same time the 50 lb wheels need to float over bumps.

  

 The shock's performance has to simultaneously satisfy two conflicting requirements: Stiff damping to control the chassis and soft damping to float the wheel over bumps. The shock can't do both, it is one or the other.

  

 The usual compromise is tune the shock to produce stiff damping at low speed for chassis control, and soft damping at high speed to control the wheels. That creates a damping profile with a “knee” in the curve marking the point where the shock transitions from low speed chassis damping to high speed wheel damping. Wipe out the knee and the chassis becomes a wallowing mess. Wrap your head around that and you will go far in suspension tuning.

  

 That's the basic theory. But on the other hand, the wheel has to punch through all of that low speed damping every time you hit a bump. Too much low speed and the ride gets harsh – not enough and the chassis wallows. Suspension tuning is all about controlling that transition and positioning the “knee” to generate enough low speed damping to control the chassis and transitioning out of that range fast enough so the ride doesn't get harsh.

  

 On the rebound side harsh means packing. Most rebound stacks use a crossover to back off on the low speed a bit and prevent packing. Go to stiffer springs and the low speed damping can be increased a bit without packing up the rebound stroke -or stated the other way – stiffer springs require stiffer rebound to keep control of the chassis and the wheels as well.

John Curea Stack Mod For Stiffer Spring Rate

 Based on the shim stack configuration and dyno test Curea suspected the rebound was too soft and recommended two mods: Increase low speed damping to handle the Dogger's 270 lb weight; and increase high speed damping to handle the Dogger's stiffer spring rates.

  

 To get more low speed damping Curea added five more face shims. The stiffer face shims kept the valve ports closed longer forcing the flow to be “on the clickers” at low speed to increase low speed damping. At high speed Curea wanted 10% more rebound damping to handle the stiffer springs. To get a 10% high speed increase Curea had to actually soften the high speed stack to compensate for the stiffer face shims. Curea's dyno test shows the combination of softer high speed and stiffer low speed results in an overall high speed damping force increase of 10%.

 [[IMG:10-curea-rbnd.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/10-curea-rbnd.png.40e16740adf50d8ac17870174b80aea3.png]]

 The stiffer stack face shims John Curea used moved the crossover closure point out to 5 in/sec compared to 3 in/sec for the Dogger's original setup. That increased the low speed damping by about 40% at 5 in/sec. At high speed the softer clamp used in Curea's stack softened the high speed damping so the overall increase at high speed was only about 10%. 

  

 Earlier, MXScandinavia tested interactive crossover stacks and showed increasing the low speed stack stiffness increased the damping force everywhere, both at low speed and at high speed. Likewise, increasing the high speed stack stiffness increased damping force everywhere across the speed range. In this test Curea shows that if you stiffen the low speed stack AND soften the high speed stack you can actually change the low speed damping force curve relative to the high speed. But you've got to simultaneously change both parts of the stack to make that work. That gets confusing if you've got a “one thing at a time” approach to shim stack tuning.

  

 The other thing John Curea shows in this test is the behavior of the crossover gap changed when the stack got re-tuned. The shaft velocity where the crossover gap closed got shifted and the abruptness or “kink” in the stack deflection curve got smoothed out. Same crossover, same crossover gap, but the two stacks are going to “feel” different on a test ride with the crossover gap transition smoothed out.

The idea of the crossover shim diameter defining the difference between active and non-interactive crossover gaps is straight forward. Implementing that in practice gets tricky because the crossover gap behavior depends on the crossover shim diameter and the stiffness of the low speed stack relative to the high speed stack. The last three dyno tests show examples where an interactive crossover gap configuration ended up testing out as non-interactive gap because the low speed stack was much softer than the high speed stack. So you have to consider both the crossover gap configuration and stack stiffness to figure out how a crossover gap is going to behave.

  

 Stack Float

 One more non-interactive crossover gap configuration from Kawamaha. This stack is for a yz250 fork compression valve. The stack uses a very thick crossover shim with a diameter that is larger than the stack clamp. That makes the stack a potential interactive crossover configuration. But, the crossover shim is very thick and stiff so it does not bend at all. That makes the configuration behave like a regular non-interactive crossover.

  

 Looking at the shim stack configuration plot it is obvious the crossover shim in that stack is not going to bend. That is not so obvious if you are just looking at a listing of shims used in the stack.

 Interactive Crossover Configurations

 MXScandinavia shows some examples of interactive crossover configurations. Interactive crossovers use a crossover shim diameter that is larger than the stack clamp. The larger crossover shim transfers some of the force on the face shims directly into the high speed stack before the crossover closes. That starts deflection of the high speed stack before the crossover closes, spreads out the closing event and shifts the crossover closure point to a higher shaft velocity.  

 [[IMG:17-ohlins-rbnd.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/17-ohlins-rbnd.png.4824e9859dca573662f09e54ab987bc1.png]]

 The final stack in that test series, mx5r, actually has a “fo” gap. That is why it falls out of family with the other stacks.

  

 On an interactive crossover the larger diameter crossover shim adds more support to the face shims making the face shims stiffer. That adds low speed damping. The larger diameter crossover shim also transfers some of the force on the face shims directly into the high speed stack. That forces the high speed stack to begin deflecting before the crossover closes and softens the crossover closure “event”. The end result is a smooth damping force curve with no distinct “kink” or event at the point where the crossover closes.

  

 There more example of interactive crossover gaps in the dyno data on TT, most of the dyno examples are actually configured that way.

MXScandinavia Jake01 Stack:

 More detail on the Jake01 dyno curve. To understand why the dyno curve is shaped the way it is you have to look at the shim stack, shim stack flow area and damping force curve to figure out what is going on in the flow circuits to chage the shape of the damping force curve. The shock tested here transitions from low speed damping, dominated by the clicker bleed circuit, to shim stack control at a shaft velocity around 5 in/sec. That is the “knee” in the low speed damping force curve.  

 [[IMG:18b-co-close.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/18b-co-close.png.b18ffb923e94059e7575e7638364f212.png]]

 At 5 in/sec the stack flow area curve shows the flow area through the shim stack is about equal to the flow area through the clicker bleed circuit. So the flow split at the “knee” is something like 50/50 between the stack and bleed circuit. As the shaft velocity advances beyond that point the bleed circuit has progressively less influence since the flow area through the shim stack is larger.

  

 Drag the slide bar on the shim stack up to the point where the crossover closes and the flow area curve shows there is about 60 lbf of fluid force on the stack. That occurs at a shaft velocity around 35 in/sec. But, drawing a straight line through the high speed stack flow area curve shows the stack does not really get into the high speed range until there is about 100 lbf of fluid force on the stack. To get 100 lbf of fluid force the shaft velocity needs to be around 65 in/sec. So the interactive crossover used in this stack has a transition range from 35 to 65 in/sec. Through that range the stack transitions from the mid-speed damping, defined by the stiffness of the face shims, to high speed damping defined by the stiffness of the entire stack.

 [[IMG:18a-co-close.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/18a-co-close.png.2bb0dbe7034445c5a3db9904e6366739.png]]

 Unfortunately MXScandinavia's dyno only had enough power to push the shock to a damping force of 600 lbf at a shaft velocity of 40 in/sec. So we never got to see how the shim stack behaved in the high speed range above 65 in/sec. To get that data the dyno would have to be able to push a shock with 1,000 lbf of damping force out to shaft velocities beyond 65 in/sec.

  

 Lack of dyno power is a common problem testing automotive shocks. It's tough to get enough power to push those shocks beyond 20 in/sec. For those situations all you get is low speed data and you can't see how the shim stack transitions into high speed damping or how the shock performs after all of the crossover gaps in the stack are closed. Data taken in that transition range can lead to all kinds of wacky dyno conclusions because the dyno is not measuring the stiffness of the shim stack, it is measuring the stiffness of the crossover closure transition and that often has little to do with the overall stiffness of the stack.

 Multiple Active Flow Circuits

 All of the shocks dyno tested on TT have compression adjusters. That means the shock has multiple active flow circuits in the compression stroke. To make comparisons with dyno data you have to combine the forces produced by the mid-valve with the force from the base valve compression adjuster along with the companion bleed circuits for each and evaluate the effect of the gas reservoir back pressure on suppressing cavitation in the flow circuits as augmented by the compression adjuster over the velocity range of the shock stroke. Combining forces from those five components gives the overall shaft force from the shock and that is the measurement made by the dyno.

 [[IMG:b7-flow-circuits.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b7-flow-circuits.png.5e0b9cfe103bfedea56b37b04fe2d514.png]]

 Those are the details – all you really do is click on the “mv_Analysis” button and the calculations combined the forces from all of those components for you to give the overall shaft damping force.

  

 In the end, you end up with a damping force from the base valve, mid-valve and the overall combined force from the shock. The MXScandinavia Jake01 dyno test, back on pg. 11, shows an example of the break down of component forces. The bulk of the damping force is produced by the mid-valve, but you are not going to match up with the dyno data until you add in the additional force produced by the base valve compression adjuster. The blue line on the plot below shows the combined damping force from the two valves. The plot also shows the data points measured for the shock during the dyno test.  

 [[IMG:b7a-flow-circuits.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/b7a-flow-circuits.png.4ca79cf492867d1ce82e5d4c47cf40bb.png]]

 Dyno testing only measures the overall damping force of the shock. To keep things simple I have only been showing that data along with the overall computed damping force. That skips over the internal details of the forces produced by each valve. Dyno data provides no way to verify those values other than they sum up to match the overall measured force.

  

 The exception is rebound data. On the rebound stroke only the mid-valve shim stack and bleed circuits are active. So interpreting dyno data on the rebound stroke you only have to figure out the flow loss through the clicker bleed circuits to get a measure of the forces produced by the mid-valve shim stack.

  

 Analysis Tools:

 As you stated ReStackor is just a tool. For the tests here the dyno has already given a measure of the shocks damping performance so there is no big mystery there. What ReStackor analysis adds to the conversation is an estimate of the shaft velocity where the crossover gap closes and the damping force events that occur at that point.

  

 There is a general expectation that the damping force kicks up at the point where the crossover closes. The data here shows that is not the case. A part of the reason for that is the shim stacks in these tests use an interactive crossover. Forces transferred through an interactive crossover cause the high speed stack to start deflecting before the crossover closes. So the face shims do not slam into a stationary high speed stack, instead the face shim kiss against a moving high speed stack and that softens the crossover closure event.

  

 The other thing to realize is the fluid flow through the shock isn't stupid. When the face shims top out against the high speed stack the fluid does not sit there and hammer against the stiff high speed stack. Instead the flow simply switches direction sending a larger portion through the clicker bleed circuits taking the path of least resistance. That further smooths out the crossover closure event so the damping force produces a smooth transition into the high speed range instead of a sharp event suggested by the sharp change in stack stiffness at the crossover closure point.

  

  

 Never-the-less I see a lot of threads around where tuners expect sharp changes in damping force at the crossover closure point. Shim stack stiffness does that, damping force does not.

 With that thinking we can come back around to the topic at hand:

How do you modify a shim stack to change the shape of the damping force curve?

If you knew how to change the curve shape what would the optimum shape be?

Kawamaha '06 yz250 fork mid-valve dyno test

  

 Kawamaha tested an interactive crossover configuration for a '06 yz250 fork rebound stack. The crossover closes at a shaft velocity of about 40 in/sec. This stack also produces a slightly digressive damping force profile.

 [[IMG:20-kawa-rbnd.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/20-kawa-rbnd.png.9ae9dc8a592b635edaf49f3843b6af95.png]]

 Since Yz295 was just talking about the uncertainty associated with interpreting dyno data from shocks due to the multiple flow circuits it's worthwhile pointing out this data from Kawamaha's hydraulic dyno feeds high pressure hydraulic fluid directly to the valve. That direct feed eliminates the uncertainty associated with interpreting flow splits between multiple active flow circuits.

  

 On the other hand, this particular test is for a rebound stack so there are only two active flow circuits (stack and bleed) and less mystery to interpret if this stack had been tested in an assembled fork on an actual dyno.
