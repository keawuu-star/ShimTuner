Shaft diameter effect on shock performance

russ17: "If you switch from a 16mm shaft to an 18mm shaft does the damping force go up or down?"

 Here's the theory:

On the compression stroke a larger shaft diameter pushes more flow through the compression adjuster (base valve) into the shock oil reservoir. That drives damping force up.

But, the larger shaft also takes up more volume in the rebound chamber so there is less oil flow through the mid-valve. That drives damping force down.

 Does a larger shaft diameter drive damping force up or down? Skoo asked that same question for a snowmobile shock.

  

 [[IMG:1yz-forces.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/1yz-forces.png.c0185fcf191c85e93a56eb71b2505862.png]]

  

 John Curea took up the challenge and dyno tested a '06 yz250 46mm kyb shock with a 16 mm and 18 mm shaft to answer russ17's question.

 Same shock, same valve, same compression adjuster

 Same shim stack

 Only change was 16 mm to 18 mm shaft

 Does a larger shaft diameter drive damping force up or down?

 [[IMG:2-yz250r-stack.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/2-yz250r-stack.png.f1ca1a6e3ac8f7208d4537a81b43662c.png]]

 The 18mm shaft pushes more fluid into the oil reservoir compressing the bladder more which increases the gas spring force measured on the dyno. To compare the 16mm shaft data to the 18mm data we need to correct the dyno measurements for the difference in gas spring force for the two shafts.

  

 All of the dyno data to this point has been PVP (peak velocity pickoff) data. PVP testing only records data at the peak shaft velocity. To get a damping force curve you have to run a series of dyno tests at progressively higher crank rpms to generate progressively higher shaft speeds only recording data at the mid-stroke position where shaft speeds are the highest. The feature of PVP testing is all of the measurements are obtained at the mid-stroke position so the gas reservoir pressure is theoretically always the same – ignoring oil temperature changes over the test which of coarse change the gas pressure.

  

 John Curea ran a CVP dyno test. CVP data (Continuous Velocity Plot) sets the dyno to run at the max shaft speed and records data over a single shock stroke. The test starts at BDC with zero shaft velocity and finishes at TDC with zero shaft velocity. Over the stroke oil is pushed into the reservoir so the final reservoir pressure at TDC is higher than the initial pressure at BDC. That creates a situation where the data recorded through the first half of the stroke does not line up with data through the second half of the stroke because of the increase in reservoir gas pressure. The change in gas force over the stroke creates a hysteresis loop in CVP dyno data.

  

 Ordinarily dyno tuners ignore the gas force change between BDC and TDC and just subtract the mid-stroke gas force as an “average” value over the stroke. That is close enough for most applications but we are trying to make some detailed comparisons here so lets make the correction one time and see how it works out. To make the correction we need to track how much oil is transferred into the reservoir as a function of stroke position, compute the gas force change, and subtract the gas force increase at each stroke position. That has been worked out in the figure below showing the original and gas force corrected data.  

 [[IMG:3-gas-force.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/3-gas-force.png.cd13a81e29b8ca191ec3bb4966dfe6a6.png]]

 Correcting the dyno data for changes in gas force over the stroke pretty much eliminates the hysteresis loop measured on the rebound stroke. Good to know.

  

 On the rebound stroke, the difference between those curves is caused by the fluid flow rate difference between a 16mm and 18mm shaft. How chose is the data to the expected effect?

Theoretical effect on rebound damping

 Dyno testing shows the 18mm shaft produced less rebound damping. That was expected since the larger 18mm shaft has less oil flow through the valve. How close are the dyno results to the expected effect?

  

 The oil volume swept through the valve is straight forward to calculate.

       [[IMG:4-yz-oil-flow.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/4-yz-oil-flow.png.4d1358b207281ef1404b6887dfa81eb5.png]]

 An 18 mm shaft sweeps an oil volume that is 3.8% less than a 16 mm shaft. That creates two effects reducing rebound damping force:  

 At any shaft velocity, the 18 mm shaft has 3.8% less oil flow through the valve. If you compared the 18mm data to the 16mm data at a shaft velocity that is 3.8% lower the oil flow through the 16mm valve would be identical

 The pressure drop across the valve is the damping force measured on the dyno. That force is created by the pressure difference between the top and bottom surface of the valve. Since the 18 mm shaft has 3.8% less exposed valve area the shock is going to generate 3.8% less damping force when the pressure drop across the valve is the same

 Both of those are simple geometric effects created by the shaft diameter change. If you corrected for the oil flow differences and the valve face area change you should be able to overlay the 16 mm shaft data on top of the 18 mm data. Those two corrections are:

 Multiply the measured 16 mm shaft velocity by (1+3.8%) so the oil flow rate through the valve is the same for both tests

 At each shaft velocity, with the same oil flow rate through the valve, the damping force produced by the 16 mm shaft is going to be 3.8% greater due to the larger exposed valve area. So you have to multiply the 16 mm shaft force data by (1- 3.8%) to correct for the difference in valve face area

 That's the theory anyway, how does it work out?

  

 Corrected 18mm Shaft Rebound Data

 The dashed blue line in the plot below multiplies each 16 mm shaft velocity data point by (1+ 3.8%) to correct for the oil flow difference and multiplies the shaft force by (1- 3.8%) to correct for the valve face area difference. With those two corrections the 16 mm shaft data should theoretically overlay on the 18 mm rebound data.  

 [[IMG:5-shaft-correction.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/5-shaft-correction.png.7937d47a2c3741f97cee327d1e69d118.png]]

 The data does not line up. After correcting for oil flow difference and valve face area the 16 mm data (dashed blue line) still produces more damping forces than the 18 mm shaft. Looks like there was more going on in that test then a simple shaft diameter change.

  

 The reason for the difference is simple I think. When kyb changed to an 18 mm shaft they took that opportunity to increase the tuning range of the clicker circuit. When John Curea swapped shafts in the dyno test that also swapped the clicker bleed circuits built into each shaft. So the dyno test ended up with different clicker bleed circuits in each shaft – that difference is important to understand. Bike model-to-model and year-to-year clicker bleed circuits are not always the same. The data here shows and example of that.

yz250 18 mm vs. 16 mm Shaft Dyno Data, Rebound

 There is no simple hand calc to correct the data for the differences in clicker flow area. Flow through the clicker circuit depends on pressure drop while flow through the valve only depends on shaft velocity. The flows are coupled, but in a non-linear way with no simple algebraic correction to relate the two. For non-linear coupled problems like that analysis tools make dyno data interpretation a whole lot easier.

  

 Put in the modified needle geometry and the computed damping force curves line up with the 16 mm and 18 mm shaft data. The 16 mm shaft produced more rebound damping force because of the larger exposed valve face area, larger swept oil volume and smaller clicker bleed circuit. We can double check the needle geometry difference by comparing the compression side data to see if the needle change lines up with that data as well.

But the larger shaft reduces oil flow through the mid-valve driving damping force down. Opposing effects make it hard to figure out if the damping force is going to go up or down with the larger shaft which is why russ17 asked the question in the first place.

 [[IMG:7-kyb46w-compression.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/7-kyb46w-compression.png.3d124c1c72ba645f0fb987f9cf22d3f8.png]]

But dyno testing only measures overall shaft force so there is no way to tell out how much of the force was generated by each component. Regardless, russ17's question was what was the overall effect of the shaft change and the dyno measures that.  

 [[IMG:8-yz250c-dyno.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/8-yz250c-dyno.png.a4928b20425db0fa1b43da3a1e63934b.png]]

But then again, the 16 mm shaft had the smaller clicker needle geometry so the difference in damping force shown by the data is a little bit exaggerated.

Oil Foaming

 The other interesting thing in John Curea's dyno data in terms of understanding shock valving is the difference in hysteresis loops between the 16 mm and 18 mm shafts. The 18 mm shaft pushes more oil through the compression adjuster. That produces more back pressure on the shock chambers which in turn suppresses the hysteresis loop.  

 [[IMG:10-yz250c-hysteresis.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/10-yz250c-hysteresis.png.f871995c66cdfb634007d4a0db2c2f9c.png]]

 You can get a better grip on the “why” of that looking at the shock chamber pressures generated by the compression adjuster. At zero shaft velocity the oil reservoir starts out with a pressure of 150 psia. As the shaft accelerates, flow through the compression adjuster back pressures the shock driving pressures in the compression chamber up. That sets the value of P.c, the green curve. Meanwhile, the pressure drop across the main piston drives pressures in the rebound chamber down (P.r, the red curve).  

 [[IMG:9-yz250-chmb-press.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/9-yz250-chmb-press.png.cb911b42010d84e01c3970d04aa76e8d.png]]

 Crank down on the compression adjuster and flow resistance through the compression adjuster goes up increasing the value of P.c which back pressures the entire shock and increases pressures in the rebound chamber (P.r). On-the-other-hand, put in a stiffer shock valve stack on the main piston and the pressure drop across the valve goes up driving pressures in the rebound chamber (P.r) down. Through tuning of the main valve compression stack and compression adjuster you can set pressures in the rebound chamber (P.r) to be any value you want.

  

 For the test here, the 16mm shaft rebound chamber pressures drop below the initial reservoir pressure and end up at a value of about 130 psia at a shaft velocity of 7 in/sec. As the shock is driven to higher shaft speeds back pressure from the compression adjuster drives chamber pressures back up so that the 16 mm shaft rebound chamber return to the initial reservoir pressure at shaft speeds around 15 in/sec.

  

 Chamber pressures with the 18 mm shaft (dashed red line) are different in an interesting way. The 18 mm shaft pushes more fluid through the compression adjuster. That produces more back pressure on the shock chambers so that the rebound chamber pressure never falls below the initial reservoir pressure. Keeping the rebound chamber pressurized over the shaft velocity ranges prevents the oil from foaming and that shows up in the dyno data John Curea measured for the shock in terms of a smaller hysteresis loop. In terms of shock valving basics that is an important concept for mx suspension tuning.

Compression Adjuster Performance

 The effect of changing the shock shaft diameter shows up in the compression adjuster performance. The 16 mm shaft data (black line) has a larger hysteresis loop then the 18 mm data (red line). The larger 16 mm hysteresis loop creates a 9% change in damping force between the first half of the stroke and the second half. The data also shows a “hitch” in the curve above shafts speeds of 15 in/sec. That 15 in/sec shaft velocity marks the point where the compression adjuster has generated enough back pressure to return the rebound chamber pressure back to the original reservoir pressure (That was shown above in the chamber pressure plot).

 [[IMG:10-yz250c-hysteresis.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/10-yz250c-hysteresis.png.f871995c66cdfb634007d4a0db2c2f9c.png]]

 After the rebound chamber gets re-pressurized at 15 in/sec the oil “heals” itself collapsing the foam bubble. That causes the 16 mm damping force to jump up closing the hysteresis loop. That behavior doesn't show up in the 18 mm data since the chamber pressures never got low enough to foam out the oil. The right hand curves re-emphasize that point showing hardly any hysteresis loop for the 18 mm data after the data is corrected for changes in reservoir pressure over the stroke.

  

 Modeling shock performance with foamed out oil has some academic interest but frankly little practical use - Here's why: Sooner or later you will come up short landing a triple. When that happens high compression velocities when the rear wheel slams into the whoop face will foam out the oil in the compression stroke. That creates the situation shown in the above Roehrig video frame with foamed out oil below the piston. When the shock flips around into the rebound stroke the piston will just be winging back through foamed out oil producing no rebound damping at all. That launches you over the bars creating your basic snake bite suspension setup.

  

 How severely the oil foams out depends on both pressure and temperature. That means it is going to be different on the first lap compared to the last lap after the shock warms up. It's also going to be different at the start of a whoop field compared to the end as the foaming progressively builds. Inconsistent behavior that depends on the instantaneous oil temperature and stroke history just isn't worth modeling or trying to figure out how to tune.

  

 The fix is simple. Tune the compression adjuster to keep the rebound chamber pressurized at or above the oil reservoir pressure over the stroke velocity range. That keeps the gas dissolved in the shock oil from foaming out. Any time the pressure drops below the reservoir pressure all of the gas dissolved in the oil comes boiling out just like opening a hot bottle of coke. The 16 mm shaft data from John Curea shows that.

  

 While all of that sounds scary, it's important to understand data from that 16 mm shaft test isn't all that bad. The oil foamed out in the 7 in/sec range, but once shaft speeds cleared 15 in/sec the rebound chamber re-pressurized and the oil fixed itself. The shock was able to fix itself because the compression adjuster was set to be much stiffer than the mid-valve. So as shaft velocities increased back pressure from the compression adjuster eventually caught up with pressure drop across the main piston and re-pressurized all of the shock chambers. That fixed the compression stroke cavitation so the rebound side showed hardly any hysteresis at all.

  

 On-the-other-hand, hack around on the compression shim stack making it stiffer and you could easily overrun the tuning authority of the compression adjuster ending up in a situation where the chambers do not re-pressurize. When that happens the oil foams out and stays foamed out. Foamed out oil can create some really wacky behavior in a shock – Valving Logic shows an example of that.

Along with a larger main piston when they made the change to the 16mm.

 A larger piston produces more damping force with a lower valve pressure drop.

 In that respect a larger piston is like switching to a larger shaft in terms of giving the compression adjuster more control over pressure balancing the shock.

  

 Penske Pressure Balancing

 Tuning the compression adjuster to keep the rebound chamber pressurized is a little different than what Penske describes for pressure balancing a shock. The Penske process, shown on [[LINK:the Roehrig site|http://roehrigengineering.com/wp-content/uploads/Pressure-Balancing.pdf]], emphasizes minimizing chamber pressures to reduce seal drag and enhance tire grip. The process recommended by Penske sets the compression adjuster so the pressure rise above the main piston matches the pressure drop behind the piston in the rebound chamber. The left hand figure shows what Penske recommends as the “Ideal balanced damper”.

 [[IMG:12-penske.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/12-penske.png.e686ca7dcc5dedb6775ed37e94595cf1.png]]

 John Curea's dyno data for an '06 yz250 shock shows you need to move towards chamber pressures in the right hand curve “Excessive head valve damper”. That configuration keeps the rebound chamber pressurized and Curea's 18 mm shaft dyno data shows that suppresses oil foaming and the hysteresis loop measured in test. Back off on back pressure from the compression adjuster using the smaller oil flow of the 16 mm shaft and the hysteresis loop starts growing. Data from that test also shows the kyb shock used in those tests did not produce a bunch of seal drag causing the dyno force to hiccup every time the shaft changed direction through the zero velocity point.

 [[IMG:11-curea-yz250-dyno.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/11-curea-yz250-dyno.png.f690dd0a5c67191eef032f8f0f6918fe.png]]

 Valving Logic shows a second example of seal drag for a yz250 shock. Valving Logic was trying to diagnose the effect of an ultra stiff compression adjuster with nearly double the back pressure driving chamber pressures up into the 1181 psi range. Comparison of the stiff adjuster to a normal compression adjuster producing about 575 psi of back pressure showed the dynamic seal drag measured in test was about the same for both configurations. Seal drag for the kyb shock used in that test series was more-or-less independent of chamber pressure.

 [[IMG:13-stiff-cmpadj.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/13-stiff-cmpadj.png.5289e5b2178941e699a915d2b4ff9c1d.png]]

 There are two ways to pressurize the rebound chamber. One is crank up the reservoir gas pressure so that the drop in rebound chamber pressures over the stroke does not go low enough to foam out the oil (Penske approach in the left hand figure). The other approach is tune the compression adjuster to dynamically back pressure the shock as shaft velocities increase (right hand figure). The cool thing about dynamic back pressure is the chamber is only pressurized when shaft velocities are high. When shaft velocities go low (like changing direction through TDC) the compression adjuster does not produce much back pressure. That keeps chamber pressures low and potentially reduces seal drag. Create back pressure by cranking up the gas reservoir pressure and seal drags are higher everywhere.

  

 On-the-other-hand the Valving Logic data shows chamber pressures do not really effect seal drag. So the bigger effect for tuning the compression adjuster is setting the rate of dynamic back pressure to keep the shock chambers pressurized and suppress the hysteresis loop.

  

 There are different schools of thought in terms of what pressure balancing a shock means. Short stroke, high frequency applications of circle track racing may require different pressure balance criteria then shocks setup for the longer strokes and higher shaft speeds of off-road applications. The data here only looks at one shock configuration and that probably does not show the whole picture.

Factory Connection High Speed Compression Spring

  

 You guys ever test the effects of a softer shock compression adjuster spring?FC sells them and wonder what they will do for dampening and cavitation

  

 Valving Logic tested a Factory Connection compression adjuster spring for an '11 kxf250 shock. To get an idea of what the FC spring does the first thing Valving Logic did was measure the stiffness of the spring:  

 Stock: 0.78 kgf/mm

 FC: 1.2 kgf/mm

 Some FC springs are stiffer – like the one tested here, others are softer. The FC kxf250 compression adjuster spring is 50% stiffer than stock.

  

 But what does that mean?

 The high speed compression adjuster spring pre-loads a plate (spring seat) that operates on the compression adjuster shim stack. Force from the spring acts on the edge of the shim stack clamp or reaches around to clamp some other shim in the stack (like the 10.2 shim in the drawing below). Either way, increasing pre-load on the spring makes the HSC stack stiffer.

 [[IMG:14-comp-adj-fig.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/14-comp-adj-fig.png.787d9a55d4ca9d43f651ff00f5139b9a.png]]

 The stock kxf250 compression adjuster has 4 turns. At 4 turns in (1 mm/turn) the spring produces a force of 3.1 kgf. The stiffer FC spring produces that same force with 2 and 5/8 turns on the adjuster. So the FC spring gives an additional 1 3/8 turns of adjustment on the compression adjuster.

  

 But what does that mean?

 To figure out what the stiffer spring does in terms of damping performance Valving Logic ran a dyno test series on the compression adjuster. The test took all of the shims off of the main piston so the compression adjuster was the only active circuit in the shock. To make sense out of the data Valving Logic had to run a second test series measuring the drag force of fluid flow through the main piston ports. That drag force was subtracted from the compression adjuster data to get a true measure of compression adjuster force.

 [[IMG:15-FC-comp-adj.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/15-FC-comp-adj.png.d6c9c38b2909585dd5a2d989e29e16ac.png]]

 At two turns out on the compression adjuster the dyno data shows the FC spring produces about 3 lbf more damping force then the stock compression adjuster.  

  

 But what does that mean?

 By itself the compression adjuster doesn't make much damping force. That surprises most people and doesn't line up with test ride results where a half turn on the compression adjuster makes a big difference in shock performance, way more than the 3 lbf shown in the above dyno data.

  

 The reason for that is the compression adjuster generates damping force in an indirect way. The compression adjuster back pressures the shock chambers and that prevents the main piston from cavitating. By suppressing cavitation the compression adjuster increases the damping force from the main piston– by a lot. Anyone who has ever cranked down on the compression adjuster could tell you that.

  

 To figure out compression adjuster performance we need to figure out how much back pressure the compression adjuster generates. To get a measure of that Valving Logic ran another dyno test series measuring the back pressure (Pc) produced by the compression adjuster for both the stock and FC spring.

 [[IMG:16-FC-chmb-press.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/16-FC-chmb-press.png.78990c7c9bad9bf7e0139f81db73d450.png]]

 Compared to the stock compression adjuster the FC spring increases pressures in the compression chamber (Pc) by about 9 psi.

  

 But what does that mean?

 So now we're getting somewhere. The FC spring generates 9 psi more back pressure. With higher back pressure the main piston shim stack can be made stiffer to produce a 9 psi higher pressure drop without cavitating the shock chambers.

 In terms of performance:

 How many face shims can you add to the main piston stack to get a 9 psi increase in pressure drop?

 How much more compression damping force would that generate?

 Add one face shim to the stack and the valve pressure drop increases by 8.6 psi. That stiffer shim stack produces about 2.6% more compression damping force. So the FC compression adjuster spring allows the main piston shim stack to be re-tuned to produce 2.6% more damping force and still keep the chambers pressure balanced.

  

 But what does that mean?

 To figure out what a 2.6% more compression damping force means in terms of suspension setup we need some way to relate spring rate, mass and damping. Spring-mass-damper theory gives that relationship in terms of the “zeta” suspension response coefficient. Keep zeta the same and suspension response is the same – that's physics.  

  

 In terms of suspension response:

 With 2.6% more compression damping how much could you increase the spring rate and keep damping performance (zeta) the same?

 With the stiffer spring how much would the rider weight increase?

 Zeta controls both low speed and high speed damping: Low speed damping controls chassis oscillations and that depends on the weight of the chassis plus rider; High speed damping controls the wheels and that depends on the un-sprung wheel weight. Zeta dictates the response of both, the only difference is using the sprung or un-sprung weight in the Zeta equation.

 [[IMG:17-zeta.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/17-zeta.png.e49c1ae84a4356ea50c20477ca83ce8a.png]]

 The compression adjuster is all about high speed damping of the un-sprung wheel weight. In evaluating zeta the wheel weight and linkage ratio are the same for both setups. That cancels out a bunch of stuff in the zeta equation. Hacking through the algebra shows high speed damping depends on a pretty simple relationship between spring rate (k) and the damping coefficient © squared.

  

 Increase high speed compression damping by 2.6% and the suspension is able to handle a spring rate increase of 5.3%. An '11 kxf250 stock suspension uses a spring rate of 5.2 kg/mm for a rider weight of 160 lb. Go over to the Race Tech spring rate calculator and a spring rate increase of 5.3% works out to a rider weight of 181 lbs.  

  

 Stiffer FC compression adjuster spring

 The stiffer FC compression adjuster spring allows you to add one face shim to the main piston stack and keep the shock pressure balanced. The stiffer damping allows the spring rate to be increase for a rider that weighs 21 lbs more. Go to a heaver rider than that and you are going to have to hack around on the compression adjuster shim stack to generate enough back pressure to handle the stiffer shim stack and spring rate of a heavier rider.

  

 To make that setup work the rebound damping needs to be increased to keep the rebound stroke zeta value is the same. Then the low speed damping needs to be increased in both circuits to handle the 21 lb increase in rider/chassis weight. Match zeta in those strokes and the suspension will bottom out, jump and damp like the stock setup with the stock rider weight – i.e. the setup the manufacturer intended for that bike. The only difference is matching zeta re-tunes the suspension for a rider that weighs 21 lbs more.

Valving Logic kxf250 dyno data

 After all of that hacking around on the dyno to figure out the effect of the FC compression adjuster spring Valving Logic ended up a pretty cool set of data with separate measurements for the compression adjuster force, main piston force and overall damping force produced by the kxf250 shock. Those component values measured on the dyno line up pretty well with the theoretical values computed for the shock.

 [[IMG:18-kxf250-1467c.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/18-kxf250-1467c.png.1dbbd20cb92dbd47878584c5c161a251.png]]

 After that initial test series Valving Logic went on to test a stiffer shim stack configuration suggested by rdmx and measured the individual shock component values for that stiffer shim stack configuration as well.

 [[IMG:19-kxf250-1469c.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/19-kxf250-1469c.png.0b4a83ac90e610e284f5b93e8805953b.png]]

 The stiffer stack ended up over running the tuning authority of the FC compression adjuster spring. That drove pressures in the rebound chamber down and caused the oil to foam out in test.  

 [[IMG:20-kxf250-pbal.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/20-kxf250-pbal.png.c0631795dfcf95af3b1d63461dd74c51.png]]

 Comparing the stiffer stack used in the above kxf250 test to the yz250 shock tested by John Curea earlier shows the chamber pressure balance difference between the two shocks. For the yz250 shock the 16mm shaft configuration allowed the rebound chamber pressures to initially drop and then recover as the compression adjuster developed more back pressure at higher speed. That initial pressure drop caused a hysteresis loop in the yz250 dyno data. The 18mm yz250 shaft never allowed the chamber pressures to drop and produced virtually no hysteresis loop in the dyno data (after correcting for pressure changes over the stroke).

  

 The kxf250 compression adjuster allows pressures in the rebound chamber to initially drop (like the yz250) but as the shaft is driven to higher speeds the compression adjuster never develops the back pressure needed to recover and re-pressurize the shock chambers. That foams out the oil and keeps it foamed out producing in some whacky behavior from that shock. In test, opening the low speed clickers from 10 to 18 caused the damping force to go up by 15 lbf! The reason for that is opening the low speed compression adjuster clicker generated even less back pressure causing the shock to cavitate harder.  The usual approach to compare valve port geometries is to look at the port area (to estimate fluid force on the shim stack) and the valve port perimeter to estimate the flow spill area.

 Valve port area

 A simple estimate for port area is the port width (w.port) times the length (d.port). For the valve port geometries given above the port area ratio works out to 1.56 for the two valves.

 [[IMG:23b-port-area.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/23b-port-area.png.7ef304c22920b51a2a90286b52514358.png]]

 With 56% more port area the Ohlins valve should have 56% more fluid force on the shim stack and that should push the shim stack to a 56% higher lift. The port area difference is a little larger than the 28% damping force difference shown by the data. But then again damping force does not linearly scale with port area.

 Port perimeter

 The spill area around the valve port perimeter is another parameter used to compare valve port geometries. Assuming some kind of triangular spill area along the d.port length of the valve spoke the ratio of perimeter spill area between the two valves works out to 1.27.

 [[IMG:23c-port-perim.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/23c-port-perim.png.e8dc57b8a9883e0cd0e9ae8540e57d74.png]]

  

 he Ohlins valve has a 56% larger port area. The expectation there is the larger port area puts more fluid force on the shim stack face causing a larger shim stack deflection. So the difference in shim stack flow area should be something like (port area)*(perimeter). Multiply that out and the ratio between the two valves works out to 1.98 which is a hole lot larger than the 1.28 damping force ratio measured in test. The magnitude is off, but at least the direction is right.

 Wave shaped shim stack deflection

 Another effect going on here is the difference in performance of a wide and narrow valve port. Those performance differences are caused by the wave shaped deflection of shim stacks, shown in the MXScandinavia finger press photo below, interacting with the valve port geometry.

 Shim stacks deflect into a wave shape. That wave shaped deflection is centered over the valve port allowing the flow to spill outward over the port edge and tangentially over the valve spokes

 On a wide valve port the wave shaped deflection only opens the middle of the valve port. The left and right edges are closed down by the wave shaped deflection. So, wide ports don't flow over the entire width of the valve port

 [[IMG:24-stack-wave.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/24-stack-wave.png.a369ace9fed5cd171af4bd27122af74f.png]]

 That puts a new dimension on shim stack tuning. Modifying the shim stack clamp, tapper or crossover gap changes the shape of the shim stack wave deflection. Those differences create situations where the shim stack may, or may not, cleanly lift over the entire width of the valve port. When the shim stack does not lift over the entire width the tuning of wide valve port geometries can get tricky.

  

 That also emphasizes shim stack lift, measured via a finger press, does not tell you if the wave shaped stack deflection cleanly opened the left and right sides of the valve port. That creates situations where two shim stacks could have the same center line edge lift but generate different damping forces because the valve port edge was open in one case but not the other.

  

 A better performance parameter is comparing shim stack flow area. That accounts for shifts in the wave deflection shape and interactions of that shape with the valve port geometry. That requires FEA analysis to integrate the flow area around the valve port perimeter or finger press measurements to provide a whole lot more detail on the tangential and radial deflection of the shim stack to get that data. Measurement of shim stack edge lift alone can't give you that info.

  

 Crossover shim stacks discussed earlier are another example where edge lift does not give the whole story, you have to look at the flow area produced by the shim stack deflection to understand how shim stack modifications are going to effect damping force.

You should read the post here again:

 Factory Connection High Speed Compression Spring

  

 But what does that mean?

 By itself the compression adjuster doesn't make much damping force. That surprises most people and doesn't line up with test ride results where a half turn on the compression adjuster makes a big difference in shock performance, way more than the 3 lbf shown in the above dyno data.

  

 The reason for that is the compression adjuster generates damping force in an indirect way. The compression adjuster back pressures the shock chambers and that prevents the main piston from cavitating. By suppressing cavitation the compression adjuster increases the damping force from the main piston– by a lot. Anyone who has ever cranked down on the compression adjuster could tell you that.

  

 To figure out compression adjuster performance ...

  

 The compression adjuster has a bunch of tuning range and it does that by controlling cavitation. So why does a softer HS compression adjuster spring reduce jarring?

  

 You mention cavitation, but it does not sound like you have really thought it through. When the compression adjuster does not supply enough back pressure to push the oil through the main valve the oil cavitates behind the main piston (Roehrig video). When the shock cavitates the oil can not make it though the main piston. So where does the oil go?

 [[IMG:A1-cavitation.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/A1-cavitation.png.268512d440db438ee9c862a99d5a94cc.png]]

 When there is not enough back pressure the oil that was supposed to go through the main piston into the rebound chamber is pushed out through the compression adjuster into the oil reservoir. We are not talking about the usual small oil flow displaced by the shock shaft. We are talking about a large oil flows displaced by the main piston – Big flows and big flows produce big pressures.

  

 Pushing that large oil volume through the small 6x2mm compression adjuster ports creates a big back pressure and that back pressure is working against the main piston area with low pressure foamed out oil behind the piston in the rebound chamber. Big pressure on the main piston area produces big forces and that creates the jarring hit MidlifeCrisisGuy is talking about on square edge hits.

  

 The 2grimjim thread linked above made 4 mods to reduce the jolt:

Increased the compression adjuster port size to 6x3.8mm. That is a 3.6 times more flow area and reduces the port pressure drop by a factor of 13

Softer FC compression adjuster spring. 2grimjim mentions the softer compression adjuster spring didn't do anything from full in to full out on the adjuster. He needed a third mod to fix that

Modify the compression adjuster spring seat to operate on the outside edge of the compression adjuster shim stack instead of the clamp shim. The modified spring seat works the same as the one SDI sells. Moving the compression adjuster spring force to work on the outside edge of the shim stack increases the compression adjuster stiffness by a lot

2grimjim also mentions he was using a modified main piston shim stack, but he does not say what that stack was

 The situation here, as I understand it, is the shock is severely cavitating on sharp compression hits. The 2grimjim mod opens up the compression adjuster ports and softens the compression adjuster spring to allow the shock to cavitate even harder and blow through the stroke on a hard hit.

  

 The other way to go, and it makes more sense to me, is just soften the main piston shim stack. That softens compression damping to accommodate square edge hits, prevents the main piston from cavitating, keeps the oil from foaming out and gives you a chance of some tunable configuration on the rebound stroke to control of the suspension motion. 2grimjim mentions he tried that approach but could not figure out how to change the main piston shim stack to keep enough low speed damping while softening the high speed.

  

 That sums up one of those basic “How do you tune a shim stack to control the shape of the damping force curve” questions. The dyno data on TT shows you how to soften high speed damping but we have not gotten to those dyno tests yet...........

 Oil Viscosity

 What the heck does DaveJ's 215.VM2.K5 – SPI-3 vibration cancellation fluid do in terms of shock performance?

  

 MXScandinavia had that question and re-tested the Jake01 stack with DaveJ's 215.VM2.K5 – SPI-3 vibration cancellation fluid to see what the oil does in terms of shock damping performance.

 [[IMG:25-spi-oil.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/25-spi-oil.png.5486a814a4d0f665cff59c5aa8c1ed62.png]]

 MXScandinavia's comment on the results:

 The SPI3 behave like a ordinary thicker oil in the beguinning but ordinary oils use to fall and meet the thinner oil in the end, but it seams that the viscosity rice with the pressure and hold the same slope angel as jake 01

 My take on MXScandinavia's comment: Push a high viscosity oil through the clicker bleed circuits and damping force goes up. But here is an interesting artifact of fluid dynamics: Once the flow goes turbulent, oil viscosity does not make much difference in flow resistance. So at high speed thin oils and thick oils end up producing about the same damping force. Whether that makes any sense or not doesn't really matter, it's just the way it is.

  

 The dyno data shows 215.VM2.K5 – SPI-3 vibration cancellation fluid does not fall off at high speed. It is stiffer everywhere, both at low speed and at high speed. Those results indicate the effect of that oil is not a viscosity effect.

  

 The other oil parameter that effects damping is oil density. It takes more force to push a heavy fluid through a flow restriction. That is a basic F=ma kind of deal and that effect is not going to drop off at high speed like oil viscosity does. Run ReStackor with a 10% higher oil density and the results line up with the MXScandinavia 215.VM2.K5 – SPI-3 vibration cancellation fluid dyno data.

 [[IMG:26-spi-rstk.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/26-spi-rstk.png.4fc19022602a60fa0a7daf37407c8d08.png]]

 Increasing the oil density effects the damping force produced from both the base and mid-valve. That alters the ability of the base valve to pressure balance the shock and control cavitation. DaveJ tries to come to grips with that in a thread here trying to figure out the fork mods needed to re-balance the chambers with the higher pressure drops produced by 215.VM2.K5 – SPI-3 vibration cancellation fluid.

  

 Brand-to-brand differences in oil density have a direct impact on damping force and those oil density differences are larger than most people realize.

 [[IMG:27-oil-density.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/27-oil-density.png.55b70277a78ae1536877c0d37fe48477.png]]

 The other important thing to get a grip on is cSt values of kinematic viscosity printed on the bottle are not a direct measure of viscosity. Kinematic viscosity is defined as the true viscosity (aka dynamic viscosity) divided by oil density.

  

 That creates problems comparing oils. An oil might have a slightly lower cSt value of kinematic viscosity (suggesting damping force would go down), but a higher density (making damping force go up). That gets even worse once you understand the higher density oil also has a higher viscosity because (true viscosity) = (Kinematic viscosity)*(density).

  

 Oil viscosity tables splattered around on the web comparing cSt values of kinematic viscosity really tell you nothing about how the oil is going to perform. You have to look at both viscosity and oil density.

When there is not enough back pressure the oil that was supposed to go through the main piston into the rebound chamber is pushed out through the compression adjuster into the oil reservoir. We are not talking about the usual small oil flow displaced by the shock shaft. We are talking about a large oil flows displaced by the main piston – Big flows and big flows produce big pressures.
  

 Pushing that large oil volume through the small 6x2mm compression adjuster ports creates a big back pressure and that back pressure is working against the main piston area with low pressure foamed out oil behind the piston in the rebound chamber. Big pressure on the main piston area produces big forces and that creates the jarring hit MidlifeCrisisGuy is talking about on square edge hits.

When fluid can’t make it through the main piston due to inadequate back pressure the rebound chamber foams out (cavitates). You seem hooked up on that idea and the Roehrig video linked above shows that really does happen in a shock. I suspect this discussion here is why that video got made in the first place. 

  

 The foam that ends up in the rebound chamber (gas bubbles) isn’t supposed to be there – that volume occupied by the foam is supposed to be filled with oil. Instead it is filled with gas bubbles and that forces an equal volume of oil to be displaced out the top of the shock.

  

 As a simple example to get your head around that idea you could take a non-cavitating shock plus some air bubbles and cram those air bubbles into the rebound chamber. Do that and the oil displaced by the air bubbles gets popped out the top of the shock. Either that or your shock body explodes due to the added volume. And no, you can’t compress the gas bubbles to zero volume. The bubbles are there, they take up space and you can actually see them.

 [[IMG:A3-volume-bal.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/A3-volume-bal.png.78943c32d161ead04bb5dbd9b2c08bf4.png]]

  

 You aren't going to be able to solve the problem by reservoir gas pressure. The Valving Logic thread you linked above shows chamber pressures in the 400 psi range at 70 in/sec. Push that out to 250 in/sec and you are going to be in the 1,000 psi range. At that pressure cavitation is not going to care much about a change in gas reservoir pressure 100 psi one way or the other.

 [[IMG:A4-chmb-press.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/A4-chmb-press.png.6afc5d226b2cc5d2dc58f4e29ccb033b.png]]

 You don’t want that, at least you say you don’t. You already have too much compression damping and that is bouncing the back end around on square edge bumps. To stop that you want less compression damping – to get there you need to soften high speed compression damping  on the main piston stack. That will soften damping and stop the cavitation. It’s a win-win situation for you.

More dyno data: Shim stack preload

 Preloading the shim stack against the valve face increases the force needed to crack the shim stack open. While the shim stack is held closed the shock is forced to blow all of the fluid through the clicker bleed circuits and that increases low speed damping - by a lot. Once enough fluid pressure is built up in the shock to overcome the shim stack preload the stack cracks open and the shock returns to normal shim stack controlled damping.

  

 MXScandinavia demonstrated the effect of preloading a shim stack by adding a ring shim to the Jake 02 shim stack.

 Change the thickness of the centering shim

Ring shim ying-yang effects

 Something to watch out for in tuning ring-shim stacks is the center section of the face shim below the ring shim is not supported. That allows the center section of the face shim to deflect and crack open before the stack edge lifts.

 [[IMG:33-ring-ying-yang.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/33-ring-ying-yang.png.4a1d94b9d2628ce9b02bd252a4a3f348.png]]

 That creates a ying-yang effect: The ring shim was added to increase low speed damping but deflection of the center section creates a leak that ends up reducing low speed damping. That creates confusion in dyno testing when the results “go the wrong way” and end up showing reduced low speed damping instead of higher. The fix is simple, put a couple more face shims below the ring shim to make the center section stiff enough so that is does not deflect.

Trouble in dyno land

  

 How accurate is shock dyno data?

  

 As an instrument, dynos make precision measurements capable of resolving shaft forces to within a fraction of a pound and shaft velocities down to the mm/sec range. As a measurement instrument there is little to complain about in the precision of dyno measurements.

  

 The problem is there are a bunch of peripheral parameters that work there way into dyno testing that create test-to-test variations and inconsistencies in damping force measurements. Those variations have nothing to do with measurement accuracy. Instead it's about uncertainties created by tearing down, re-configuring, purging, and zeroing a shock that create damping force differences in back-to-back tests and discrepancies comparing one shock to another.

Shim defects

 Ideally all of the shims in the stack are perfectly flat. In reality shims are warped, cupped, bent or pretzeled with irregular edges from being punched out of a sheet of shim stock. Tightening the clamp nut compresses all of those defects preloading the stack face shim. Dirt or grit trapped in the stack creates another problem. Nobody assembles these things in a laminar flow clean room. Instead everything is spread out on the work bench and dragged around creating conditions where dirt occasionally gets trapped in the stack. If that grit is inside the clamp diameter the shim stack has a gap making the stack softer. It the grit is outside the clamp the grit particle preloads the stack face shim.

Trapped dirt and grit

 Dirt trapped in the shim stack changes damping force more then you might expect. Wikipedia defines “very fine sand” as a grain size of 62 to 125 microns and silt as 4 to 62.5 microns. Lets skip sand and just look at a single particle of silt with a 30 micron diameter. Put that particle in MXScandinavia's 14x40.2 stack and the difference between a 30 micron gap created by the silt particle trapped under the clamp and 30 microns of shim stack preload created by a silt particle outside the clamp produces a 20% change in damping force.

 A single silt particle trapped in the shim stack changes damping force by 20%

 On a fielded shock trapped dirt in the stack is not a big deal. That dirt gets ground up and spit out after a couple of hours of operation. Things are different on the dyno. Dyno tests are run just long enough to get the data – like a couple of minutes. Over that short duration there is not enough time to grind up trapped dirt and that results in measurement variations when comparing back-to-back tests.

Shim friction

 The Aerospace Corp and Professor Darling at the University of Bath have both noted shim stack are 17% stiffer when the shim stack is pushed open compared to forces measured on a finger press when the shim stack is lowered back to the closed position. Those differences are caused by shim friction and directly translate into another source of uncertainty in dyno damping force measurements.

  

 Professor Darling went on to note flooding the shim stack with oil minimized the friction differences. Add to that vibrations generated by turbulent flow through the shock circuits and friction effects may or may not effect the results of a test. All of the theoretical damping force curves in this thread have used the nominal (normal) value of shim friction and have not considered friction as a cause of test-to-test variations. Never the less the effect exists and is another source of uncertainty when comparing the measured performance of one shock to another.

Valve face dish

 Dyno comparisons often skip over differences in valve face dish when comparing shock performance. Most manufactures dish the valve face to something in the range of -0.001”. The valve face dish preloads the shim stack and sucks up the uncertainties of warped, cupped, bent or pretzeled shim defects giving the shim stack a good seat on the valve face. You have to look really close to see a valve face dish of -0.001”. For perspective, a sheet of paper has a thickness of 0.0039”, four times thicker. 

 [[IMG:2-concave-surface.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/2-concave-surface.png.3b26074eaa623a8b41862fece5aad19a.png]]

 Some tuners remove the valve face dish under the notion of perfecting the valve seat surface. Flattening the face occasionally creates a problem when the leading edge of the valve digs into the grinding stone. That grinds off the outside edge so the valve ends up with a convex surface and no edge sealing at all – remember we are talking about tolerances here like 1/4 the thickness of a sheet of paper.

  

 Depending on who got their hands on the valve, the valve seat may be flat, concave or convex. Taking the MXScandinavia 14x40.2 stack as the reference again and putting an uncertainty of +/- 0.0005” on the dish depth generates a 13% change in damping force. Softer stacks can dyno test stiffer depending on which valve they were tested on.

Shock configuration differences

 The usual assumption comparing shock dyno graphs is everything in the shock is identical except for the items intentionally changed for the test. That is not always the case. John Curea provides an example in dyno testing a yz250 shock. The assumption was swapping the shock shaft diameter from 16 mm to 18 mm would show the effect of the larger shaft on damping force. It turned out the clicker needle built into the 18 mm shaft was different then the 16 mm shaft. So the damping force differences measured in test got exaggerated by the needle geometry differences between the two shafts.  

  

 Dyno tests do not always record the details of clicker needle geometry, compression adjuster configuration or gas reservoir bladder volume or pressure. That can screw up shock-to-shock dyno comparisons not because the data was bad, but because there were differences in the shock configurations due to year-to-year changes or internal modifications nobody knew about when testing the shock.

Dyno test procedure differences

 The unambiguous dyno verified damping force curves for a shock are measured by one of two dyno test procedures:

 CVP: Continuous velocity plot. Dyno data is recorded over a single stroke starting at BDC, stroking through TDC and returning to BDC. Over the stroke the gas force from the shock is continuously changing as the gas reservoir is compressed and re-expanded

 PVP: Peak velocity pick-off. The dyno only records data at the peak shaft velocity which occurs at the dyno mid-stroke position. With all of the measurements taken at mid-stroke the gas force is theoretically always the same. To construct a damping force curve over the shaft velocity range a series of dyno runs have to be made at progressively higher crank speed

 CVP dyno measurements show 20% to 30% less damping force then PVP measurements for the same shock tested on the same dyno as Valving Logic shows in the data below.

 [[IMG:5-pvp-vs-cvp-dyno.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/5-pvp-vs-cvp-dyno.png.b250956fba0619785bc4836b908c9a5c.png]]

 At 40 in/sec the CVP test results match the PVP data -as it should- since both of those measurements where taken at the mid-stroke position where the peak shaft velocity occurs. Data at that position lines up by definition. At any other stroke position the CVP dyno data measured less damping force.

  

 The reason for that is the gas force from the shock increases as the gas reservoir is compressed over the stroke. To make use of CVP data the CVP data has to be corrected for the change in gas reservoir pressure over the stroke. Correcting CVP data for gas force (as demonstrated in John Curea's yz250 shock testing here and here) collapses the hysteresis loop measured in the data making the CVP data independent of stroke position. Correct the CVP data for the gas pressure at mid-stroke and the PVP data will line up with the CVP data by definition.

  

 Unfortunately, standard dyno test procedures do not correct CVP data for gas reservoir pressure changes over the stroke. That creates discrepancies in damping force measurements in the 20 to 30% range as Valving Logic demonstrated in the above test. BTW the CVP data above only shows the compression open part of the stroke.

Gas spring force corrections

 Push Industries shows a second example of problems created by ignoring gas force changes over the stroke in EMA dyno testing of a '14 yzf450 shock. The change in gas force from BDC to TDC is clear in the EMA dyno test data and the gas force produces a 40 lbf offset at zero shaft velocity.

 [[IMG:6-ema-dyno.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/6-ema-dyno.png.ae412f0ab49b8228a132652eda122349.png]]

 Take the dyno data literally (and why wouldn't you) creates a 35% error in low speed damping force measurements. Plot the data as a damping coefficient and the first half of the stoke from BDC to mid-stroke produced a progressive damping force curve (blue curve). The second half of the stroke from mid-stroke to TDC produced a digressive damping force curve (green curve). Using the raw data directly off the dyno makes it impossible to figure out if that shock produced a progressive, digressive or linear damping force curve. 

 [[IMG:7-ema-error.png|//cdn.thumpertalk.com/uploads/imageproxy/7-ema-error.png.ffad48b290f45444fff89b14d6b2d241.png]]

 There is nothing “wrong” with the Push Industries EMA dyno data. All CVP dyno data behaves this way. The results simply emphasizes you can not take raw CVP dyno data directly off the test stand and make use of it. The data has to be “post-processed” to correct for gas reservoir pressure changes over the stroke. If you don't do that you end up with 35% measurement errors at low speed and confusion trying to figure out if the damping force curve was progressive, digressive or linear.

  

 Dynos make a correction for the average gas force measured at the shock mid-stroke position. But do not correct for the changes in gas force from BDC to TDC. Those corrections are the users responsibility to add into the data when interpreting and comparing dyno tests. Those corrections are in the range of 20 to 35% at low speed and become a big deal when you start worrying about dyno measurement accuracy.
