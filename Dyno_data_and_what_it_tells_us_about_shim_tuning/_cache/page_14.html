          Thank you for posting this, might be a little beyond my current suspension knowledge

          but it gets my curiosity going into further experimenting.

           

          Most difficult part is pinpointing exactly what these changes do on track.

           

          Here's what I do:

          Charts are around relating suspension speed to bump height. Those charts give an idea of where different bump heights put you on the damping force curve.

           

          A 1” bump generates a wheel speed of about 100 in/sec. Put that on a 3:1 linkage and the shock would be running at about 33 in/sec shaft speed.

           

          The idea you can just look at a damping force curve and tell what the shock is going to “feel” like is nuts.

          The best you can do is compare one setup to another. Damping force curves with the clicker range on there help get a little more quantitative about how the differences are going to “feel” on a test ride. In this case the clicker range is +/- 5 clicks.

           

          The “new” stack on that plot is going to “feel” about the same on a 1” bump since the damping force is about the same. The difference is that new stack is about 5 clicks stiffer at low speed and goes soft at high speed. With that info you can make a decision whether that stack is worthwhile or if you are looking for some other stack with a different damping force curve shape.

           

          The idea here, following GHILL28's question, is all of these stack mods are "what if" questions. Make the change, plot the results, and see if the stack mods are changing damping force in the velocity range you are interested in. If not, look at the stack structure, figure out how the stack is deflected in the velocity range you are interested in and then hack around on the stack structure to get the change you want.

          So on the topic of translating dyno data to real world events through the bump size/shaft speed relationship, has anyone ever validated this data to include the deflection of the tire using live instrumentation on the bike?  Obviously the shaft speeds would shift downwards a little bit, and I'd expect a proportionally greater drop at higher vehicle speeds until which speed the tire is hitting the rim.  Just wondering if there is an approximation for that amount of shift - what amount of shaft speed the tire compression is responsible for taking up.  I realize it's PSI/casing/rim size dependent.  Probably another topic in itself.

           

          Those suspension velocities are computed for square edge bumps. There is no tire out there with a sidewall stiff enough to keep the tire off the rim on a sharp edge bump. But if you are worried about it, just take an inch off, or whatever, from the bump height and bracket the range.

            

          The purpose of that chart is getting a grip on the upper range of suspension velocities. With 12 inches of travel and 4 inches of race sag, bump travel is 8 inches. At some sane speed like 50 mph that puts the upper range of suspension velocity at 550 in/sec. Put that on a 3:1 link suspension and the max shaft velocity on the shock is 180 in/sec.

           

          The suspension community produces a bunch of whining about the worthlessness of dyno data in off-road applications due to the extreme suspension speeds. That whining revolves around the notion of non-linear shim stack behavior generated by the extreme stack deflections and speeds of off-road applications. The dyno data on TT – real data from real shocks - shows that is not the case.

           

          In the data here MXScandinavia measured shim stack stiffness at suspension velocities of 1,500 in/sec. That is beyond the suspension velocity range of that chart! The data showed a regular stiffness progression predicted by FEA with a smooth curve and stiffness progression initiated at low speed. No unexpected behavior there.

           

          MXScandinavia also showed some of the Ohlins factory data run out to shaft velocities of 120 in/sec. That data showed no unexpected behavior – just a smooth progression of damping force. But there is a gap. There is no data between 120 in/sec and peak off-road velocities of 180 in/sec. In that gap you have got to be careful designing shocks to insure the shim stack does not hit the backer, the valve ports don't restrict the flow or generate some unexpected cavitation event.

          That Ohlins factory data is some very special dyno data you are not going to pick up from your local tuner. TT is lucky to have it along with the corresponding MXScandinavia finger press data showing nothing unexpected happens in those shim stack configurations when pushed to ultra high deflections.

           

          The more typical situation for high end dyno testing limits shaft velocities to 60 in/sec, like the Valving Logic data. The more common case is 20 in/sec like MXScandinavia and John Curea. Extrapolating that data to higher shaft speeds you have to really think about what is going on in the shock and shim stack when pushed to higher speeds. That is not always obvious when looking at nothing but the low speed data. Some of the “fo” gap tests coming up here provide examples where the crossover is “fo” over the range tested, but when pushed to higher speeds the crossover closes, the stack becomes stiffer, and the damping force curve changes into a shape that is a little different then expected based on the lower speed dyno data.

           

          The idea the damping force curve changes when the crossover closes is not a big mystery – but it is if tuning the shock using nothing but the dyno data and the dyno was not able to generate the shaft speeds needed to close the crossover gap. That generates the classic situation where the shock suddenly becomes stiffer at high speed and the dyno data does not line up with the realities of a test ride.

          Max Rebound Stroke Velocity

          Using the link ratios from adynes in the force balance the wheel weight slows down the rebound stroke by about a factor of two. The other thing that is interesting is once the spring gets the wheel moving the shock damping can't slow it back down. So the final velocity at full extension is higher than the shaft only test kawamaha ran.

          Peak rebound velocities are going to change with stroke length and the damping force. To get a handle on the velocity range in the rebound stroke I reran a couple of cases with suspension response coefficients (zeta values) from 0 to 0.9, just short of critical damping. Over that range the peak rebound velocities went from 100 to 270 in/sec. For the kxf450 link ratio used in this example, the shock shaft velocity went from 50 to 100 in/sec – that's the shaft velocity range you need dyno data over to figure out rebound response.

          For tuning off-road suspension systems the shock shaft velocity range is:

          • Compression damping: 180 in/sec, 8” bump at 50 mph
          • Rebound damping: 50 to 110 in/sec, free extension velocity depends on damping

          For tuning forks those shaft velocities are going to be a factor of three higher since there is no link ratio.

          Rebound Velocity

          What does a stock suspension generate for peak rebound velocities? John Curea dyno tested a stock '06 yz250 shock on TT. The rebound stroke data is plotted below.

          Rebound stroke shaft velocities are going to be in the 50 to 110 in/sec range, but that dyno data only goes to 20 in/sec. Where is that curve going at high speed?

          The data shows the damping coefficient starts kicking up around 10 in/sec. That is not unexpected since the high speed in the shim stack looks pretty stiff. With that thinking, extrapolating the data slope out to higher speeds might be a reasonable guess. That gives a damping coefficient around 70 lbf-s/in at 100 in/sec.

           

          On the other hand, experience shows damping coefficients tend to roll over at high speed as fluid pressures balances out against shim stack stiffness. That forces the damping coefficient to roll over into a more or less constant value at high speed. But that 20 in/sec data shows no evidence of the curve rolling over and no way to guess the shaft velocity where that might happen.

           

          With no data beyond 20 in/sec you just don't know what that curve shape is going to be and there is no easy way to guess. In the test-test-test approach the usual thinking is use what you got, the data shows a max value of 27 lbf-s/in so just use that – no guessing.

           

          Run that shim stack through ReStackor and you get a little better picture of where the damping coefficient curve is going at high speed. The stack is progressive out to shaft velocities around 50 in/sec and then rolls over, as most stacks do, into a more or less constant high speed value. So the crossover in that stack did not snap closed, it kind of eases into it and as the face shims mash out against the high speed stack the damping force curve slowly rolls over into a more-or-less constant high speed value, something around 30 lbf-s/in – not 70 lbf-s/in from extrapolation of the low speed data.

          So what does that mean in terms of rebound response? We've got three cases here:

          1. Damping coefficient measured in test: 27 lbf-s/in
          2. Calculated high speed value: 30 lbf-s/in
          3. The actual damping coefficient curve. We need to run the whole curve to see how the damping coefficient drop off at low speed effects stroke velocity

          Peak rebound shaft velocities turned out to be around 100 in/sec as expected. The peak velocity is sensitive to the damping coefficient used. The difference between 27 lbf-s/in and 30 lbf-s/in generates a peak suspension velocity difference of around 8 in/sec and that effects how long it takes the suspension to reach full extension – i.e. rebound packing.

           

          To get a handle on packing, I plotted the distance traveled “in the air” at 30 mph for the suspension to reach full extension. That works out to 7.5 feet for the dyno value of 27 lbf-s/in and 8.5 feet for the stiffer guess of 30 lbf-s/in. So it's about a foot more air time.

           

          Run the whole damping coefficient curve (the “Lookup” curve) and you can see the suspension velocity kick up as the shock drops into the low speed range at the end of stroke. Interestingly, that does not effect the ground distance traveled much as it only comes into play over the last two inches of stroke. Stated the other way around, low speed damping makes a big difference in suspension packing when the suspension is bouncing around the +/- 2 to 4 inch stroke range, but does not make much difference when rebounding off the suspension stop.

           

          Darren from Push Industries explained it to me like this.  A development program needs three legs: data acquisition, dyno testing, and field testing.  You can learn a lot from each one individually, but they build on each other when combined.  It makes perfect sense when you think about it, and it is no surprise that's how all well-funded auto and road race teams do it.  This is finally trickling down to motocross, but we are still a long ways behind.  My data acquisition stuff still isn't finished but as I promised before I'll post some information about bump and rebound wheel velocities when I have something to report.  Thanks to Clicked for keeping this thread going.

           

          The data here provides one of those examples where it is hard to guess high speed damping behavior based on nothing but low speed data. Some of the “fo” crossover dyno tests coming up provide additional examples of things to watch out for and examples where it's easy to misinterpret dyno data when extrapolated. Those are situations where the physics based approach of therory-test-analysis provides the three legs needed for data interpretation. You just aren’t going to get that out of a test-test-test mentality.

          The hard part of rebound damping curves for shocks is finding the feeling of planted (slow rebound at low shafts speeds - minimal bleed) with the traction of lots of bleed.

          KTM trax is their solution to this.

          The application of throttle - and bike geometry and effects of chain force are VERY important here too.  On many suspension systems - up to a point - the application of power drives the rear tire DOWN - and front end DOWN if not for wheelie tendencies.

          But at a certain cross over poing in the stroke - often this chain force pulls the tire UP.  

           

          Learning the motorcycle as a rider and how to best utilize this - takes lots of seat time

          How a bike handles ON the gas - and off - can vary HUGELY which immensely effects shock set up. We've found the kawasaki 450 a good example bike for this.

           

          Edit:  Something of note:  Clicked alluded to finding the shaft velocity due to a bump - then crossing that over to the dyno data of two separate shock stacks that were of similar damping at that shaft velocity.  Id like to point out that, up to that shaft speed, and decreasing from that shaft speed would be very different between those two stacks - and as such would NOT feel the same or respond the same despite having the same damping at peak shaft speed of the bump.

          Suspension Link Ratio


          adynes posted two link ratios: one for a kxf450 and the other for a kx250. Does changing the suspension link ratio change suspension response?

          There are three ways to change a link:

          1. Put in the new link and don't worry about it
          2. Put in the link and adjust the spring rate to get race sag right
          3. Put in the link and spring, then re-tune damping to get the suspension response right

          Whats the difference?

           

          Lets assume the setup on the kxf450 works, but you've heard using the kx250 link is the hot setup. How does the kx250 link ratio effect suspension performance?

          Step one: Tossing the link in there and trying to get the setup working without setting race sag is ridiculous. We're skipping step 1.

           

          Step two: the higher kx250 link ratio needs a stiffer spring to get race sag right. Put in the stiffer spring and the shock is now under damped. That causes the suspension to bong out to full extension and hit the stop at high velocity as shown in the plot below. To keep that setup from bonging you over the bars that stiffer race sag spring needs stiffer rebound damping.

          Step 3: To get damping right the shock needs to be re-tuned so that the damping force at the wheel, after operating through the modified link ratio, produces the same force as the original kxf450 setup. That comes down to getting the same zeta value. Re-tune the shock compensating for the higher link ratio and the suspension response ends up being pretty much line-on-line with the original kxf450 setup. The modified link ratio, after getting race sag and damping right, did not change much of anything in the suspension response or “feel”.
