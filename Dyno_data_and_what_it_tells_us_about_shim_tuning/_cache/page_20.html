          Conventional wisdom is the damping force curve shape can be tuned by hacking around on the low or high speed stack. After kicking this title around for 20 pages “Dyno data -And what it tells us about how to tune a shim stack and control the shape of the damping force curve” the dyno data has shown that conventional approach does not work.

           

          Stiffer low speed stack

          Adding shims to the low speed stack increases the damping force everywhere, not just at low speed. MXScandinavia demonstrated that in dyno testing shim stack configurations suggested by mog (mog01 and mog02) designed to test the low speed stack theory.

          Adding shims to the low speed stack increases damping force everywhere, not just at low speed.

           

          Stiffer high speed stack

          Adding shims to the high speed stack increases the damping force everywhere, not just at high speed. Ohlins factory dyno data demonstrated that effect.

          Stiffening the high speed stack increases damping force everywhere, not just at high speed. The conventional thinking of a separate low and high speed stack just does not work out when those theories are compared against dyno data.

          How do you change the shape of the damping force curve?

           

          It's real simple:

          • If you want less low speed damping add a crossover

          • If you want more low speed damping pre-load the shim stack

          • If you want more high speed stiffen the shim stack

          Reduce low speed damping

          Adding a crossover softens low speed damping. Valving logic demonstrated that effect dyno testing stacks with and without a crossover.

          Adding a crossover softens damping, that is not a big surprise. The problem is the crossover also softens high speed making the damping force softer everywhere. So to soften low speed damping you have to do two things:  

          1. Add a crossover to soften low speed damping

          2. Stiffen the shim stack to get high speed damping back to where is was

          Increase low speed damping

          MXScandinavia showed how to increase low speed damping in dyno tests by preloading the shim stack using a ring shim. The problem is the preload increased damping force everywhere, not just at low speed.

          To increase low speed damping you have to make two changes in the shim stack at the same time:

          1. Add a ring shim to increase low speed damping

          2. Soften the shim stack to get high speed back to where it was

          Damping force curve shape tuning

          The above dyno tests demonstrate three basic damping force curve shapes and the shim stack configurations for each:  

          1. Simple tapered stack for a linear damping force curve

          2. Crossover shim stack to reduce low speed damping

          3. Ring shim preloaded stack to increase low speed damping

          The tricky thing for tuning is there is no independent control of low and high speed damping. If you want more low speed damping you have to do two things: preload the shim stack AND soften the shim stack. Otherwise the stack just gets stiffer everywhere. Likewise a crossover reduces low speed damping, but you have to do two things: add a crossover AND increase the shim stack stiffness to get high speed back in the ballpark. Otherwise the stack just gets softer everywhere.

           

          That makes dyno tuning on the test stand a PIA. If you want to increase low speed damping by 10% you have to add a ring shim to preload the stack and then hack around through a couple of dyno tests to get the right ring shim thickness and preload to match the 10% target. After that a second series of dyno tests is needed softening the stack to get the high speed back to where it was. At the end, that softer stack needs a couple more dyno tests hacking around on the thickness of the ring shim to get low speed damping back to the 10% target. That may end up taking 7 to 10 dyno tests and gets expensive at $50 a stroke on the dyno.

          Damping force shape tuning secrets

          Shim stack preload and crossover gaps give an unlimited tuning range once you understand how to use them. In the limit a crossover can have a single face shim and produce basically zero low speed damping. Changing the gap height controls when the high speed stack kicks in and tuning the high speed stack stiffness controls how stiff that is going to be. Alternatively large preload on a stiff shim stack will hold the shim stack closed, hydraulically lock the shock, making the shock infinitely stiff.

           

          Between those limits of zero and infinity there is a damping force curve that is going to work. But you have to know how to manipulate the shim stack to get those curves. That gets a little complicated because changing the damping force curve shape requires simultaneous changes to the crossover gap or stack preload AND the stack stiffness. You can't get there only changing one thing at a time in the usual tuning approach.

          I'm looking at working on a preload stack to get low speed,bleed shim to get rid of ugly spike before shim stacks open with a soft single stage for less hs

           

          The only reason to preload a shim stack is to increase low speed damping. If that ends up creating too much low speed you either have a shim stack that is too stiff or too much ring shim preload.

           

          This preload discussion got started by MidlifeCrisisGuy looking for a way to soften high speed damping for square edge bump compliance. The problem he and 2grimjim had is they could not figure out how to soften high speed and keep enough low speed damping to prevent the chassis from wallowing around. Their solution was disarm the compression adjuster, imo cavitation from that mod causes other problems.

           

          It's easy to understand their frustration:

          • The stock stack had a crossover. By conventional thinking the crossover should soften low speed and get stiffer at high speed after the crossover closes

          • To get something with the same low speed and less high speed the fix should be a straight stack. With nothing but face shims a straight stack should be as stiff as possible at low speed. Using a straight stack and backing off on the stiffness should give less high speed damping and the maximum possible low speed

          Something like this: Starting with the rmz450-1415c stack tested by Valving Logic.

          The above example shows the straight stack ends up with less low speed damping than the original crossover stack. Those results imply there is no way to back off high speed without loosing low speed damping. So you are stuck.

           

          Add a ring shim and things are different. With preload from the ring shim a softer stack can match the low speed damping of a stiffer stack and get softer at high speed simply because the stack is softer. For example:

          Getting that to work takes some tuning:

          • Too much preload and you are going to end up with an ugly low speed damping force spike

          • Too stiff of a stack and there is going to be an ugly damping force spike at low speed and too much damping at high speed because the stack is stiffer

          To get something that works it's got to be the right stack stiffness with the right preload. Mess up either one of those and it is not going to work. You can soften low speed damping a little bit with clicker adjustments or a valve port leak jet if you have to. But, either one of those mods is going to muck with rebound damping. So you would have to go fiddle with the rebound stack to make it a little stiffer, compensating for the added bleed, to get rebound back to where it was.

           

          The only reason to put a ring shim in the stack is to increase low speed damping. If that ends up producing too much low speed damping and an ugly low speed spike you either have a shim stack that is too stiff or a ring shim with too much preload. You need to back off on one or the other – no reason it has to be ugly.
