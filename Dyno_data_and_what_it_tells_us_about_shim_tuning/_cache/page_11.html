          Interactive Crossover Configurations

          MXScandinavia shows some examples of interactive crossover configurations. Interactive crossovers use a crossover shim diameter that is larger than the stack clamp. The larger crossover shim transfers some of the force on the face shims directly into the high speed stack before the crossover closes. That starts deflection of the high speed stack before the crossover closes, spreads out the closing event and shifts the crossover closure point to a higher shaft velocity.  

          The final stack in that test series, mx5r, actually has a “fo” gap. That is why it falls out of family with the other stacks.

           

          On an interactive crossover the larger diameter crossover shim adds more support to the face shims making the face shims stiffer. That adds low speed damping. The larger diameter crossover shim also transfers some of the force on the face shims directly into the high speed stack. That forces the high speed stack to begin deflecting before the crossover closes and softens the crossover closure “event”. The end result is a smooth damping force curve with no distinct “kink” or event at the point where the crossover closes.

           

          There more example of interactive crossover gaps in the dyno data on TT, most of the dyno examples are actually configured that way.

          MXScandinavia Jake01 Stack:

          More detail on the Jake01 dyno curve. To understand why the dyno curve is shaped the way it is you have to look at the shim stack, shim stack flow area and damping force curve to figure out what is going on in the flow circuits to chage the shape of the damping force curve. The shock tested here transitions from low speed damping, dominated by the clicker bleed circuit, to shim stack control at a shaft velocity around 5 in/sec. That is the “knee” in the low speed damping force curve.  

          At 5 in/sec the stack flow area curve shows the flow area through the shim stack is about equal to the flow area through the clicker bleed circuit. So the flow split at the “knee” is something like 50/50 between the stack and bleed circuit. As the shaft velocity advances beyond that point the bleed circuit has progressively less influence since the flow area through the shim stack is larger.

           

          Drag the slide bar on the shim stack up to the point where the crossover closes and the flow area curve shows there is about 60 lbf of fluid force on the stack. That occurs at a shaft velocity around 35 in/sec. But, drawing a straight line through the high speed stack flow area curve shows the stack does not really get into the high speed range until there is about 100 lbf of fluid force on the stack. To get 100 lbf of fluid force the shaft velocity needs to be around 65 in/sec. So the interactive crossover used in this stack has a transition range from 35 to 65 in/sec. Through that range the stack transitions from the mid-speed damping, defined by the stiffness of the face shims, to high speed damping defined by the stiffness of the entire stack.

          Unfortunately MXScandinavia's dyno only had enough power to push the shock to a damping force of 600 lbf at a shaft velocity of 40 in/sec. So we never got to see how the shim stack behaved in the high speed range above 65 in/sec. To get that data the dyno would have to be able to push a shock with 1,000 lbf of damping force out to shaft velocities beyond 65 in/sec.

           

          Lack of dyno power is a common problem testing automotive shocks. It's tough to get enough power to push those shocks beyond 20 in/sec. For those situations all you get is low speed data and you can't see how the shim stack transitions into high speed damping or how the shock performs after all of the crossover gaps in the stack are closed. Data taken in that transition range can lead to all kinds of wacky dyno conclusions because the dyno is not measuring the stiffness of the shim stack, it is measuring the stiffness of the crossover closure transition and that often has little to do with the overall stiffness of the stack.

          All of the dyno tests above have links to the original threads so you can check out the dyno data for yourself. It's real dyno data from real shocks.  

           

          For the shocks tested here 30 HP is not going to get you to 3.5 m/s. As an example the Ohlins factory dyno data above produced 3,000 lbf of damping force at a shaft velocity of 80 in/sec. That works out to 30 HP delivered at the shock shaft at 2 m/s. But that test was only at 2 m/s, pushing the shock out to 3.5 m/s is going to require nearly double the HP you're talking about.  

           

          Extending the shock travel does not make sense to me. Power is force x distance/time. Extending the travel to 6” just requires you to push the same force through a longer distance. But with the longer stroke you get more time to do it. In the end 1000 lbf of damping force at 2 m/s requires the same power on a 4 inch or 6 inch stroke. 

           

          For you extended stroke setups could you show us some of those shocks? I would like to see how you adapted the reservoir pressurization system and shaft bleed circuits on the longer shock body.

           

          This thread is all about swaping data and trying to figure out what it means.

          Remove Rebound Stack:

          What GD170 is getting at is shocks are tuned to produce more force on rebound than compression.

           

          When your dyno bumps against the power limit you can extend the test range a bit by removing the rebound stack. That makes compression damping the limiting load, and sense that is softer, you can push the dyno out to a slightly higher shaft velocity – but you're only getting data on the compression side. That technique mucks up the shock operation a bit because high chamber pressures generated in the rebound stroke don't get a chance to “clear” foamed oil from the shock.

           

          As a dyno owner / operator I am curious as to how much of this data is extrapolated mathmatically vs real world? Very little of this data actually looks like it is dyno generated as opposed to computor generated.

          Our dyno operates at 3.5 metres per second  at 150mm of stroke with nearly 30 hp behind it. In order to test shocks at this speed we have had to have shocks made with longer travel in both 46 and 50mm configurations.

          We have an enormous amount of data on hand. It is surprising to see exactly how much effect shim thickness actually has compared to the supposed mathmatical value. There is a huge anomaly here.

           

          Race Tech Extended Shock Bodies:

          Matthew Wiley, Race Tech's Operations Manager, put out some info on the G3-S extended shock bodies Terry Hay is using for dyno testing. From the Race Tech info:

          "What we have found is the adjusters on these G3 shocks, based on dyno testing, have a much much greater percentage, or range of adjustability, then anything else that is presently available on the market."

          "Extended adjustability" means Race Tech drilled out the bleed circuits in those G3-S shocks to get the clickers to operate over a wider range. Testing kyb 46mm or 50 mm valves in those shock bodies is going to produce a damping force curve very much different than OEM because of the modified bleed circuits.

           

          Terry Hay: I think you need to look at this problem the other way around. You say your dyno results “look” different than the theoretical mathematical curves and the dyno data everybody else is measuring for these shocks. To resolve those differences I think you need to take a hard look at the bleed circuit configurations you have in those G3-S shocks and shaft and try to figure out what you can do to modify those circuits to emulate the OEM setup. If your damping force curves don't lineup with the OEM curve you are just plain old testing something different. Worse, the shim stack configurations developed in those G3-S shock bodies are going to be worthless to an OEM shock because of the bleed circuit configuration differences.

           

          If you can't get the OEM valve with the OEM stack to line up with the OEM dyno data, those G3-S shock bodies just aren't going to be very useful for shim stack tuning.

          the max. rebound velocity is given by the spring preload, so I guess less rebound velocity (but more rebound force)?

           

          Exactly.  You specify the rebound and compression velocities, and the machine outputs the force required to achieve those velocities.  On the bike, max rebound velocity is less than max compression velocity, so testing a symetical sine wave at high speeds affects the compression stroke in ways that would not occur in the real world.  Cavitation on the rebound stroke causes more severe hysteresis on the compression stroke.  I don't know what real-world max rebound velocities are yet, but I'm working on it.  I'm still very new to this machine so please accept my explanation with caution.

          I've always felt two types of dynos are needed for the "budget" conscious.

          In order to hit the velocities we want - without huge sustainable HP...an impact type single shot dyno would be neat.

           

          Use a large amount of potential energy with an adjustable linkage into the shock and be ready to record the "damping" decay

           

          Then a conventional rotary type that is common for most dyno owners who aren't donald trump to get bleed and transition data.

           

          For a low budget approach I've got to give some prop's to Kawamaha and his hydraulic dyno. With a high pressure hydraulic pump and pressure gauges Kawamaha can get some really good measurements of the pressure drop across the piston as a function of shim stack configuration and valve design at velocities beyond conventional dyno testing. That's all steady state stuff, but it's high quality, accurate and repeatable.

          Hydraulic testing will not give you the transient response or the effect of back pressure interactions from the base valve/compression adjuster in a shock. All of that is important stuff, but it take a whole lot more equipment and expense to make those transient measurements.
