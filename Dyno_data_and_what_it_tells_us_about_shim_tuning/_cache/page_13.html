          Kawamaha '06 yz250 fork mid-valve dyno test

           

          Kawamaha tested an interactive crossover configuration for a '06 yz250 fork rebound stack. The crossover closes at a shaft velocity of about 40 in/sec. This stack also produces a slightly digressive damping force profile.

          Since Yz295 was just talking about the uncertainty associated with interpreting dyno data from shocks due to the multiple flow circuits it's worthwhile pointing out this data from Kawamaha's hydraulic dyno feeds high pressure hydraulic fluid directly to the valve. That direct feed eliminates the uncertainty associated with interpreting flow splits between multiple active flow circuits.

           

          On the other hand, this particular test is for a rebound stack so there are only two active flow circuits (stack and bleed) and less mystery to interpret if this stack had been tested in an assembled fork on an actual dyno.

          • How do you modify a shim stack to change the shape of the damping force curve?
          • If you knew how to change the curve shape what would the optimum shape be?

          This thread is focused on the first step, compiling dyno data demonstrating how to go about changing the damping force curve shape. After we get a handle on that and the practical range damping force curves can be tuned over we can start the debate over what the optimum curve shape might look like. 

           

           

          I prefer to do a little reverse engineering here. Test to find out what the shortcomings are and roughly what percentage change we are looking for in what areas. Dyno the shock. Combine the data. Next pull it down making the appropriate changes and test again. Have we done enough? Have we done too much? Have we nailed it. Once we are happy that we have the bike working really well, we can dyno the shock again.

          A larger/smaller clamp or thinner/thicker shims in the high speed & (in forks, valve stack "float" ) would affect the high speed portion.

          More/less or thicker/thinner face shims would change the overall stiffness of the curve.

          Crossover gap or diameter to alter the transition/arc from low to high.

          KPRacing:

          Thanks for the input. Those are the classic shim stack tuning rules. Through comparison with dyno data we've found those rules only apply to the special case of a non-interactive crossover. For anything else the rules don't work and that does not seem to be widely known. That is how this thread got started.

          • "A larger/smaller clamp or thinner/thicker shims in the high speed & (in forks, valve stack "float" ) would affect the high speed portion."
            • No. The MXScandinavia dyno data here and here shows increasing the high speed stack stiffness makes the damping force stiffer everywhere, not just at high speed. To understand “the why” of that we adopted Jeremy Wilkey's definition of interactive and non-interactive crossovers.
            • Interactive crossovers have a shim diameter larger than the stack clamp. That allows force transfer directly through the crossover shim into the high speed stack before the crossover closes. Direct force transfer makes the stiffness of the low speed stack a function of high speed stack stiffness. Under those conditions changes to the high speed stack or clamp end up effecting low speed damping as well.
          • ​"More/less or thicker/thinner face shims would change the overall stiffness of the curve."
            • True that. We've also found a tall stack of thin shims is more progressive than a short stack of thick shims setup to be shim factor equivalent. That creates some discrepancies when A/B'ing damping force values from shim stacks with different stack heights.
          • ​"Crossover gap or diameter to alter the transition/arc from low to high."
            • Increasing the crossover diameter will make low speed damping stiffer. At some point, increasing the crossover diameter will shift the stack from a non-interactive to interactive crossover and chang the stack behavior. Further increases will eventually cause the crossover to become “fo” and the gap will do nothing at all.

          Every one of those comments above are nit-picky little details and I apologize for that. But the thing is, stacking up the rules against dyno data has shown all of those nit-picky little details come into play and end up generating dyno results that just don't line up with the expectations of the standard shim stack tuning rules. Those differences generate discrepancies when compared against the reality of a test ride.

           

          Getting a handle on the cause of those discrepancies gets complicated. I think the combination of theory, test and analysis applied in this thread has demonstrated some progress on getting to “the why” of those discrepancies and made some progress on identifying the range of shim stack configurations where those standard rules don't apply.

          Valving Logic rmz250 dyno testing

          Valving Logic dyno tested an interactive crossover in a thread here. The stack tested is from mog and uses a complex staged interactive crossover on a Showa 50 mm shock valve. The large crossover gap at the bottom of the stack creates a closing “event” in the shim stack port edge lift curve at a fluid force of around 70 lbf on the stack. The fluid force on the stack does not reach that level until the shock shaft velocity is beyond 100 in/sec.

           

          In the dyno test the maximum shaft velocity was 70 in/sec, so effects of closing the lower crossover gap were never seen in the dyno data. Over the tested range the shim stack produced a nearly linear damping profile.

          All of the interactive crossover gap shim stack configurations tested by MXScandinavia produced digressive damping force profiles. The stack tested here produced a linear damping force profile demonstrating an important point. Interactive crossovers do not necessarily produce a digressive damping force curves. The shape of the curve depends on the stiffness of the low speed stack, high speed stack, stack clamp diameter and path forces transferred through the crossover shim. How each of those four components interact within the stack structure is hard to guess and that makes it hard to guess if the stack is going to be progressive, digressive or linear.

           

          The other important thing, and it makes sense after you look at the stack, the top crossover gap closed first, followed by the bottom crossover at a higher shaft velocity. So the stack did not start deflecting at the outside edge and peel back as is commonly thought. Instead, the entire bottom structure deflected forcing the upper crossover gap to close. Then the low speed stack mashed out against the high speed stack eventually forcing the lower crossover gap to close last at a higher shaft velocity.

          Trapped Crossovers

          Trapped crossovers use a pair of shims with different diameters to form the crossover gap. As the crossover closes the larger shim gets trapped in the closing crossover gap. The trapped shim changes the effective clamp diameter on the face shims forcing the stack to become stiffer as the crossover gap closes.  

          MXScandinavia Jake Normal Stack

          The MXScandinavia Jake-normal dyno test demonstrates use of a trapped crossover. Comparing test results of the Jake-normal dyno test to a conventional crossover used in the Jake 01 stack demonstrates the effect of a trapped crossover. Both dyno tests were run on an Ohlins four port MX valve.  

          Accept for the crossover, the two stacks are identical. The Jake01 stack uses a single 28.15 mm shim to form the crossover. The Jake-normal stack uses a pair of 34.1, 28.1 mm shims to create a trapped crossover. By the thickness cubed rule the single 0.15mm shim in the Jake01 stack is 1.7 times stiffer than a pair of 0.1mm shims. In terms of shim stiffness the Jake01 stack should be stiffer.

           

          In terms of gap: the pair of shims in the Jake-norm crossover creates a 0.2 mm crossover gap. The single shim in the Jake01 stack creates a 0.15 mm gap. The smaller crossover gap should make the Jake01 stack stiffer.  

          • A single 0.15mm shim is stiffer than a pair of 0.1 mm shims making the Jake01 stack stiffer

          • The smaller 0.15mm crossover gap makes the Jake01 stack stiffer

          • Dyno test shows the opposite result

            • The theoretically stiffer Jake01 stack dyno tests softer

          Theoretically, the Jake01 stack should be stiffer because the single 0.15mm crossover shim is stiffer and the crossover gap is smaller. But the dyno test shows the opposite result, the theoretically stiffer Jake01 stack turns out to be softer.

          The cause of the backward result is the pair of crossover shims used in the Jake-normal stack forms a trapped crossover. As the crossover closes the larger 34.1mm crossover shim gets trapped in the closing crossover gap. The trapped shim shifts the effective clamp diameter on the face shims making the face shims stiffer and causes the theoretically softer Jake-normal stack to dyno test stiffer.

           

          While that sounds complicated, looking at how the stack deflects makes it pretty easy to understand how a trapped crossover works. The trapped shim simply transfers more force into the high speed stack.  

          The above test is a good example where three things (theory, test and analysis) are needed to make sense out of the data. Those three are like the pillars of a three legged stool, each supports the other. Data is needed to validate theory, theory to develop analysis tools and analysis tools to understand the data. Working together those three create a sum that is greater than the parts.

           

          In this case the Jake01 stack is clearly stiffer in terms of shim stiffness and crossover gap. In that sense the test data makes no sense. Take it to the next level applying the more thorough theories of FEA stack deflections and the action of the trapped crossover forcing the Jake-normal stack to become stiffer makes sense, which in turn validates the test data – and makes sense out of the test data that initially made no sense at all.

           

          Got to give some “cred” to MXScandinavia for not throwing that test away as a simple example of bad dyno data.

          Valving Logic Split Crossover Testing

          Many shim stacks use a split crossover pair and don't form trapped crossovers. Valving Logic shows some examples.  

           

          The first two configurations, rmz250-54c and kxf450-753c, were tested on a 46 mm kyb valve. The shim pair used to form the crossover gap in the rmz250-54c stack have similar diameters. In that configuration the lower shim does not reach far enough into the crossover gap to become trapped. The next stack, kxf450-753c, demonstrates the same thing with the lower crossover shim pretty much at the limit of just kissing the high speed stack when the crossover closes. That shim is almost trapped, but not enough to make a difference in stiffness of the stack.

          The third configuration demonstrates the same thing but in a different way. The rmz250-875c stack, tested on a Showa 50 valve, uses a shim pair with different thicknesses. The thicker top shim creates a larger crossover gap and does not trap the lower shim even though the diameter of that shim is much larger reaching far into the crossover gap.

           

          The above stacks demonstrate classic examples of the “it depends” exceptions to crossover tuning. The stacks tested use split crossover pairs with staggered shim diameters. So the stacks “could” behave as trapped crossovers. But the specifics of the stack configuration, shim diameter ratio and thickness of the upper shim relative to the bottom shim, end up preventing the stacks from behaving as a trapped crossover.

           

          Many shim stacks use a split crossover pair for the simple reason of softening low speed damping. By the thickness cubed rule, a pair of 0.1 mm shims if four times softer than a single 0.2 mm shim. So replacing a 0.2 mm crossover shim with a pair of 0.1 mm shims makes the stack softer. Staggering shim diameters of the crossover pair also spreads out the corner load on the face shims so the shims don't kink when bending around the sharp corner of a single crossover shim. In that sense using a staggered pair of crossover shims makes the stack structure more robust.

           

          With that in mind, replacing a 0.2mm crossover shim with a pair of 0.1mm shims should make the stack slightly softer. The MXScandinavia trapped crossover test shows the important exception where a split crossover gets trapped in the gap and actually ends up making the stack stiffer. So, “it depends”.

          Valving Logic kxf250 Shock Compression

          Another dyno example from Valving Logic of a trapped crossover. This test was run on a Showa 50mm valve for a kxf250 shock. Two stacks were tested: One with a conventional crossover and the second using a split crossover pair.

          By the thickness cubed rule of shim factors the kxf250-1467c stack should be stiffer at low speed because the thicker pair of 44.25 face shims are 1.95 times stiffer than the 44.2 face shims used in the kxf250-1469c stack. The kxf250-1467c stack also uses a larger crossover shim diameter with a thinner crossover gap. The combination of stiffer face shims and a smaller crossover gap should make the kxf250-1467c stack stiffer at low speed.

           

          But the kxf250-1469c stack uses a trapped crossover and that crossover is pushing against a much stiffer high speed stack. So after the face shims lift 0.1mm to close the 26.1 crossover the trapped 36.15 shim is going to be working against a much stiffer high speed stack and clamp potentially making that stack stiffer.

           

          So is the split crossover used in the kxf250-1469c stack stiffer or not?

          Both FEA analysis and the dyno data show the two stacks were close at low speed but the split crossover pair in the kxf250-1469c stack was slightly softer. The crossover in that stack closes earlier, transitions into the high speed range sooner and becomes stiffer at high speed. All of those differences were expected, but not big enough to overcome the larger crossover gap of the split crossover pair. The larger crossover gap ends up making the kxf250-1469c stack softer at low speed. That might have turned out different if the stack had used a 36.1/26.1 crossover pair to match the crossover gap of the kxf250-1467c stack.

          "fo" Crossovers

          Four different crossover styles are demonstrated in the dyno data posted on TT. The final style is a “fo” crossover. Like “fo” fur, “fo” crossovers are fake and don't do anything but cause confusion determining if the crossover does anything or not. “fo” crossovers are created by interactive crossover shim diameters that are too large or a high speed stacks that are too soft. In either case, the high speed stack deflects at the same rate as the face shims so the “fo” gap never closes. “fo” gaps really screw up test-to-test comparisons when crossovers are assumed active but end up producing the same damping force as a simple tapered stack with no crossover at all.

           

          The MXScandinavia 14x40.2 stack, used to test shim factors at the top of this thread, is an example of a “fo” gap. Over the velocity range tested the crossover gap never moved. That stack was tested to shaft velocities of 120 in/sec (9.1 m/s wheel speed) which is equivalent to hitting a 4 inch bump at 50 mph. High speed stuff, yet the impact produced no motion of the crossover gap. Take it further and apply several hundred pounds of force to the stack and you can get the crossover gap to close but at that point the stack is deflected so far the valve port throat area is controlling the flow, not the shim stack.
